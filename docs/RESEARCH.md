# Research Record — ADHD, music learning, and reinforcement

Verified literature behind the learning engines. Generated from a multi-agent review (five literature areas researched in parallel, every claim then adversarially fact-checked by an independent reviewer that attempted to refute it).

**How to read the status tags.** `CONFIRMED` = the citation exists and the claim represents it accurately. `MISCHARACTERIZED` = the paper is real but the claim overstated it — the correction is quoted. `UNVERIFIABLE` = the citation could not be confirmed; do not build on it. Evidence strength: `STRONG` = replicated or meta-analytic, `MODERATE` = one well-powered study, `WEAK` = small-n, children-only generalised to adults, or non-peer-reviewed.

**Rule for using this file:** only `CONFIRMED` findings at `MODERATE` or `STRONG` may drive a parameter change. Everything else is context.

See `docs/LEARNING.md` for the resulting architecture and the tunables each of these findings justifies.

## Teaching music/piano to people with ADHD — peer-reviewed evidence base, with emphasis on adults, for an adaptive practice-app algorithm

### Adults (not just children) with ADHD show a large, robust deficit in tracking a musical beat — both perceptually and sensorimotorily — that is not explained by general motor variability. Adults with ADHD were impaired on the Beat Alignment Test (d = 1.20) and on paced tapping consistency (d = 1.39). Crucially, the deficit is heterogeneous: roughly 31–38% of ADHD participants performed within the control range. Authors interpret it as failure to generate an internal beat (basal-ganglia/striatal timing circuitry), and explicitly recommend rhythm-based intervention.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Puyjarinet, F., Bégel, V., Lopez, R., Dellacherie, D., & Dalla Bella, S. (2017). Children and adults with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder cannot move to the beat. Scientific Reports, 7, 11550.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5599521/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Cross-sectional case-control. ADULTS INCLUDED: 21 adults with ADHD (19.1–50.1 yrs, M=31.4) vs 18 matched controls (19–42 yrs). Plus 55 children (22 ADHD, 19 ADHD+DCD, 14 controls). Methylphenidate withheld at testing. Tasks: paced tapping to tones and music at IOIs of 450/600/750 ms, unpaced tapping as motor-variability covariate, Beat Alignment Test, anisochrony detection.
- **Effect size:** Adults: BAT t(37)=3.50, p<.001, d=1.20; paced tapping consistency t(33.2)=4.34, p<.0001, d=1.39. Children BAT-music d=1.19; duration discrimination d=1.31. Group effect survived covarying unpaced motor variability: F(1,67)=31.06, p<.00001, ηp²=.32.
- **Transfer to this app:** High transfer. This is the single strongest adult-inclusive finding in the literature and it is directly measurable with the app's own MIDI input. The caveat is that it describes tapping/perception, not two-handed piano repertoire, so the calibration is a scoring-tolerance prior, not a prediction of piano performance.
- **Design implication:** Do not treat timing error as a proxy for inattention or laziness, and do not ship a single global timing tolerance. Run a one-time calibration (paced tapping at 450/600/750 ms + a beat-alignment discrimination probe) at first launch and store a per-user synchronization-consistency baseline. Derive the note-onset scoring window from that baseline rather than a fixed ±ms constant, and widen it further at faster tempi. Because ~1 in 3 will be unimpaired, the calibration must be able to return 'no adjustment needed' — never apply a blanket ADHD handicap.
- **Verifier notes:** Every specific verified against the full text. Adults: 21 ADHD (19.1–50.1 yrs, M=31.4, SD=10.5) vs 18 controls (19–42.2, M=32). BAT: t(37)=3.50, p<0.001, d=1.20. Paced tapping consistency d=1.39. 'When covarying for motor variability, ADHD children and adults still revealed lower synchronization performance than controls.' Heterogeneity verified but the claim blurs it: the paper reports 31.4% of CHILDREN and 38% of ADULTS within the control range — two separate figures, not a range across one pooled sample. Methylphenidate withheld for the week preceding testing in adults. Authors do write 'rhythm-based interventions ... may be particularly valuable in ADHD.' Children: 41 ADHD (22 ADHD-only + 19 ADHD+DCD) + 14 controls = 55, matching. STRENGTH CAVEAT: the word 'robust' in the claim is unearned. The adult cell is n=21 vs 18, single lab, and I found no independent adult replication of these effect sizes. d of 1.20–1.39 at that n has very wide confidence intervals and is exactly the regime where effect sizes inflate. The 38%-unimpaired figure is itself estimated from 21 people (~8 individuals), so the design premise '~1 in 3 will be unimpaired' rests on a point estimate whose interval plausibly spans 20–60%. Direction credible; magnitude unreliable.

### Music training produces a moderate-to-large improvement in inhibition control in children — a genuine far-transfer effect, and larger than that of comparison activities (sports, visual arts, drama). Across 8 RCTs, SMD = 0.63 (95% CI 0.41–0.85, p<.0001); across the fuller set of 20 longitudinal studies including quasi-experimental and passive-control designs, SMD = 0.36 (95% CI 0.21–0.50). Effects were observed with training doses in the range of roughly 480–960 minutes over 12–16 weeks.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · STRONG
- **Citation:** Jamey, K., Foster, N. E. V., Hyde, K. L., & Dalla Bella, S. (2024). Does music training improve inhibition control in children? A systematic review and meta-analysis. Cognition, 252, 105889.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39197250/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Systematic review + meta-analysis, records 1980–2023: 22 longitudinal controlled studies, N = 1734; 8 RCTs. CHILDREN ONLY (notably 4–8 year olds in the strongest recent RCTs). No adult samples.
- **Effect size:** RCT subset SMD = 0.63 (0.41–0.85), p<.0001; full longitudinal set SMD = 0.36 (0.21–0.50), p<.0001.
- **Transfer to this app:** Moderate, with an honest gap: every included study was children, mostly with teacher-led group instruction. The dose figures are a defensible planning anchor; the claim 'this app will improve your executive function' is NOT supported for an adult self-directed user and should not appear in copy.
- **Design implication:** Use total accumulated on-instrument minutes as the primary long-horizon progress metric, and target a first-milestone dose in the evidenced range (~500–1000 minutes of active playing, i.e. roughly 30 min × 4–5/week × 12 weeks). Show cumulative active-minutes as a blunt counter. Also: this is evidence for privileging *active production* (playing/producing rhythm) over passive listening or theory modules — which aligns with the spec's anti-theory-prerequisite stance.
- **Verifier correction:** Effect sizes and design are exactly right: SMD=0.63 (CI 0.41–0.85, p<.0001) for the 8 RCTs; SMD=0.36 (CI 0.21–0.50, p<.0001) for the 20-study superset; 22 longitudinal controlled studies, N=1734, records 1980–2023. THE DOSE FIGURE IS NOT IN THE PAPER. The meta-analysis reports that 'over 300 minutes of training in varied environments yield inhibition control improvements' — a minimum threshold, not a 480–960 minute window — and its moderator analysis found training INTENSITY DID NOT MODERATE transfer, with effects present regardless of age, intensity, or instruction method. The '480–960 minutes over 12–16 weeks' range appears reverse-engineered from included trial protocols rather than reported as an evidenced dose range. Also the article number is 105913, not 105889.
- **Verifier notes:** The 'privileged role compared to other activities (sports, visual arts, drama)' wording is verbatim from the abstract, so that part is fair — but it is an author conclusion drawn from the music-vs-active-control contrast in the RCTs, NOT a head-to-head meta-analytic comparison of sports vs arts vs drama. No such subgroup analysis was run. Strength rated STRONG for the child inhibition finding itself (8 RCTs, negligible heterogeneity, no publication bias detected), but the population is children ~3–11 with the strongest RCTs in 4–8 year olds and there is NO adult evidence. Far-transfer of instrument practice to inhibition control in an adult is unevidenced by this paper. The design implication ('cumulative active-minutes as primary metric, target 500–1000 minutes') therefore rests on (a) a dose range the paper does not report and (b) an age extrapolation it does not license. The ~300-minute figure is the only defensible anchor, and even that is a narrative inference rather than a fitted dose-response curve.

### A gamified, at-home, tablet-delivered rhythm training program is feasible and adherable in an ADHD population, and within that protocol LONGER individual sessions were associated with GREATER timing gains. The trained group also showed preliminary evidence of improved executive functioning/inhibitory control versus an active control game matched on auditory-motor demands but lacking beat synchronization.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Jamey, K., Laflamme, H., Foster, N. E. V., Rigoulot, S., Lippé, S., Kotz, S. A., & Dalla Bella, S. (2025). Can you beat the music? Validation of a gamified rhythmic training in children with ADHD. Behavior Research Methods. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-025-02802-3
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-025-02802-3
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** SMALL-N feasibility/validation RCT. 27 children with ADHD, ages 7–13, randomized to 'Rhythm Workers' finger-tapping beat-synchronization game vs an active control game without beat synchronization. At-home, unsupervised, delivered across Canada. Dose: 300 minutes total over 2 weeks (~30 min/session, 5 sessions/week). CHILDREN ONLY; n=27 is too small for confident effect estimates.
- **Effect size:** Not extractable at a reliable level from available full text; reported as 'preliminary evidence' of EF improvement. Treat as feasibility-grade, not effect-size-grade.
- **Transfer to this app:** Good structural transfer (at-home, tablet, unsupervised, gamified, self-administered — the same delivery modality as Fingerly), weak effect transfer (n=27, children, 2-week window). Use it for dose and loop design, not for outcome claims.
- **Design implication:** Two concrete algorithm decisions. (1) Default session target ~30 minutes at ~5 sessions/week, and when the user is engaged, let the session run longer rather than cutting at a timer — the dose-response within this protocol favoured longer sessions. (2) The active-control comparison is the design lesson: the differentiating ingredient was beat synchronization, not general auditory-motor busywork. So the app's core loop should require the user to align production to an external pulse (metronome/backing pulse) rather than merely hit correct pitches at self-chosen timing. Score alignment-to-pulse as a first-class metric alongside note accuracy.
- **Verifier correction:** The paper reports that children who played Rhythm Workers 'showed improved general RHYTHMIC ABILITIES compared to the controls, and these improvements were positively correlated with the PLAYING DURATION' — i.e. with TOTAL time played across the two-week protocol, not with the length of individual sessions. Because total dose was fixed at 300 minutes by protocol, variance in playing duration is adherence/engagement variance, making this an observational within-group correlation confounded with motivation and baseline severity — not a randomized or planned dose manipulation. Nothing supports 'longer individual sessions → greater gains.' 'Timing gains' also overstates 'general rhythmic abilities.'
- **Verifier notes:** Design details all verify: 27 children aged 7–13, randomized to a finger-tapping rhythmic game vs a control game 'with comparable auditory-motor demands but without beat synchronization,' 300 min over 2 weeks, at-home and unsupervised across Canada. 'Executive functioning improved in those who played RW, but not in the controls' — correctly labelled preliminary. The active-control contrast is the study's real strength, and the design lesson drawn from it (require alignment to an external pulse; score alignment as first-class) is legitimate. But the specific decision derived from the dose-response — 'let the session run longer rather than cutting at a timer' — is not supported. With n=27 split two ways (~13/arm) and a correlational dose analysis, this is proof-of-concept only, children 7–13, no adult data.

### In ADULTS with ADHD, adding immediate performance-contingent reward normalizes specific deficits rather than uniformly boosting performance. Reward eliminated the ADHD group's timing-reproduction deficit and abolished their delay aversion (rewarded ADHD ≈ non-rewarded controls), and reduced CPT false positives. But it did NOT improve stop-signal omissions or n-back accuracy, and it *slowed* median reaction times in the ADHD group — i.e. reward shifted the speed/accuracy tradeoff toward caution rather than improving raw capacity.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Marx, I., Höpcke, C., Berger, C., Wandschneider, R., & Herpertz, S. C. (2013). The impact of financial reward contingencies on cognitive function profiles in adult ADHD. PLoS ONE, 8(6), e67002.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3688618/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** ADULT SAMPLE. 38 adults with ADHD vs 40 controls, ages 18–40; randomized between-subjects reward vs no-reward across six computerized tasks (stop-signal, n-back, CPT, time discrimination, time reproduction, delay aversion). Small per-cell n (~19–20).
- **Effect size:** Time reproduction: length-dependent error growth present without reward, F(6,204)=6.35, p<.001, ηp²=.16; absent with reward, F(6,222)=0.94, p=.47. CPT false positives: F(1,35)=6.89, p=.01, ηp²=.16. Slowed RT under reward in ADHD: t(36)=2.00, p=.05. Null for stop-signal omissions and n-back.
- **Transfer to this app:** High transfer on population (adults with ADHD) and mechanism (immediate contingency). Lower transfer on reinforcer type: this was real money, not points. Also note the spec's ban on cheerleading copy is compatible — the effective ingredient was informational immediacy of a contingent outcome, not praise.
- **Design implication:** Attach immediate, per-attempt contingent scoring to every practice repetition — most importantly to the TIMING dimension, because timing/interval reproduction is the specific function reward normalized in adults. Never batch feedback to end-of-session only; delay is the aversive variable. Two guardrails: (a) because reward slows responding, do not couple reward to tempo — reward accuracy-at-target-tempo, and raise tempo on a separate schedule, or the user will trade speed for score; (b) reward did nothing for working-memory load, so do not expect an incentive layer to rescue passages that are too long to hold in memory — fix those by segmenting instead.
- **Verifier notes:** Every element verified verbatim. 38 adults with ADHD vs 40 controls, 18–40 yrs, 'randomly assigned to the reward conditions' (between-subjects). Time reproduction: reward 'minimized absolute discrepancy score increases in subjects with ADHD and equalized the performance between the groups.' Delay aversion: rewarded ADHD 'did not demonstrate different performance compared with controls in the non-reward condition.' CPT false positives: 'higher ... in the non-reward condition but not in the reward condition.' Nulls: 'stop-signal omission errors, n-back accuracy and response time variability in the CPT were not affected by rewards.' Slowing: 'subjects with ADHD exhibited longer reaction times in the reward condition than in the non-reward condition, whereas controls did not,' interpreted as a speed/accuracy strategy shift. ONE OMISSION WORTH ADDING: CPT reaction-time VARIABILITY was also unaffected by reward — directly relevant here, since RT variability is the closest analogue to timing consistency in a piano app, and reward did nothing for it. That tempers the implication that per-attempt reward will improve timing consistency specifically. SKEPTICAL CAVEATS: per-cell n ~19–20; six tasks with multiple dependent measures each and no evident multiplicity correction, so the exact PATTERN of which functions normalized is the kind of dissociation that often fails to replicate; the authors themselves flag possible ceiling effects on the delay-aversion task. Single study, unreplicated.

### Background music's effect in ADHD is load-dependent and can reverse sign: it improved arithmetic, simple reading comprehension, upright balance, motor restlessness and negative mood, but DEGRADED performance on challenging reading tasks — consistent with overstimulation once cognitive load is high. Optimal Stimulation Theory was only partially supported, and calm music outperformed rhythmically salient music. The review also confirms children with ADHD show timing-perception and beat-tracking deficits while rhythmic improvisation and musical expression remain intact.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Saville, P., Kinney, C., Heiderscheit, A., & Himmerich, H. (2025). Exploring the Intersection of ADHD and Music: A Systematic Review. Behavioral Sciences, 15(1), 65.
- **URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/1/65
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** PRISMA 2020 systematic review; 20 studies (1981–2023), 1,170 participants (830 with ADHD/ADD), 12 countries, 5 RCTs, JBI quality appraisal. PREDOMINANTLY CHILDREN/ADOLESCENTS — the authors flag only ONE adult music-listening study (Zimmermann et al., 2019) and ZERO adult music-therapy studies. Six included studies had n<20; ~75% of participants had comorbidities; medication reporting inconsistent.
- **Effect size:** Narrative synthesis; no pooled effect size reported.
- **Transfer to this app:** Mechanism transfers plausibly to an adult (arousal/load interaction is not developmentally specific), but the evidence base is almost entirely children. The 'strip accompaniment on hard material' rule is cheap to implement and low-risk even if the finding is child-derived.
- **Design implication:** Make any auditory enrichment (backing track, accompaniment, drum groove, ambient pad) a function of current task difficulty rather than a constant. Concretely: full backing texture for drilled/consolidated material and for warmup, but strip to a bare click — or silence — the moment the algorithm classifies a segment as 'new/failing' (high cognitive load), since that is exactly where the review found music hurts. Second implication: since expressive/improvisational capacity is intact while timing perception is impaired, include a free-play or improvisation mode as a low-failure re-entry path, and do not gate it behind timing accuracy.
- **Verifier notes:** Citation exact. All substantive elements verify: 20 studies (1981–2023), 1,170 participants (830 with ADHD/ADD), 12 countries, 5 RCTs, JBI appraisal. Degradation confirmed — 'music had a negative impact on more difficult reading tasks' and 'music might distract students with ADHD when the cognitive load of a task is high.' OST support explicitly partial (Abikoff 1996, Madjar 2020 supportive; Aydinli 2018, Greenop & Kann 2007 'questioning the credibility'). Calm vs rhythmic confirmed: 'calm music, with or without lyrics, was most effective ... whereas rhythmic music had a lesser impact.' Intact abilities confirmed: 'adolescents with ADHD and ADD scored similarly to controls in rhythmic improvisation and musical expression.' ERROR IN THE SUBMITTED SAMPLE/DESIGN FIELD (not in the claim itself): '~75% of participants had comorbidities' misreads the review, which cites the general epidemiological figure that '75% of individuals with ADHD have a co-occurring mental health disorder'; the review did not aggregate comorbidity rates across included samples. 6 of 20 studies with n<20 verified. Adult evidence essentially absent — one adult music-listening study (Zimmermann 2019), zero adult music-therapy studies. STRENGTH WEAK: narrative systematic review with no meta-analysis; the load-reversal finding rests on individual small studies rather than pooled estimates, and 'intact improvisation' traces to a single adolescent study. The load-adaptive backing-track implication is a reasonable design bet, not an evidence-based requirement.

### Hyperfocus — long, deeply absorbed attention episodes — is MORE frequent, not less, in adults with higher ADHD symptomology, and the highest-frequency setting is hobbies. Adults with elevated ADHD symptoms reported higher total and dispositional hyperfocus and more frequent hyperfocus in school, hobbies and screen-time settings, plus on a real-world-scenario subscale. Later validation work found the dispositional measure correlates positively (weakly) with flow.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Hupfeld, K. E., Abagis, T. R., & Shah, P. (2019). Living "in the zone": hyperfocus in adult ADHD. ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders, 11(2), 191–208. (Follow-up validation: Ashinoff & Abu-Akel-adjacent line; Hupfeld-derived AHQ-D validated in Scientific Reports, 2024, r(345)=0.12 with flow.)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30267329/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** ADULT SAMPLE, two independent samples: pilot n = 251 and replication n = 372 adults with and without ADHD. Preregistered hypothesis. CAVEAT: cross-sectional SELF-REPORT questionnaire study using a newly created instrument (Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire), ADHD indexed by symptom scales (Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scale) rather than clinical diagnosis in all cases. Correlational; no behavioural measure of attention duration.
- **Effect size:** Group differences reported across subscales; magnitudes are questionnaire-scale differences, not standardized behavioural effect sizes. AHQ-D–flow correlation r = 0.12 (small).
- **Transfer to this app:** Directly on-population (adults with ADHD) and on-context (hobbies is the highest-hyperfocus setting, and a piano app is a hobby). But it is self-report only, so treat it as a design permission ('don't cap engaged sessions') rather than a prescriptive rule.
- **Design implication:** Do not hard-terminate a session on a clock. The spec correctly forbids open-ended sessions, but the resolution is goal-bounded rather than time-bounded: define the session by a completion criterion (N segments at criterion tempo), then, when the criterion is met while the user's recent accuracy trend is still flat or rising, offer a single explicit opt-in extension block with its own stated goal. Conversely, log session-length distribution per user and use their own observed engaged-bout length to size the default block, rather than a population default. Never interrupt a rising-accuracy streak with a mandatory break prompt.
- **Verifier correction:** The core finding is exactly right — 'those with higher ADHD symptomology reported higher total and dispositional HF and more frequent HF across each of the three settings (school, hobbies, and screen time) as well as on a fourth subscale describing real-world HF scenarios' — and the follow-up correlation is exact: r(345)=0.12, p=0.028, described in the AHQ-D validation as 'a weak positive correlation between AHQ-D scores and flow.' But I could NOT verify that hobbies is the highest-frequency SETTING. The paper reports the high-symptom group scoring higher across all three settings; it does not rank the settings against one another, and no source I checked reports hobbies as the top-scoring subscale. Drop that sub-assertion. The citation is also partly garbled: 'Ashinoff & Abu-Akel-adjacent line' is not a citation (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel 2021 is a separate conceptual review in Psychological Research); the AHQ-D validation is a distinct 2024 Scientific Reports paper. The 2019 paper additionally carries a published Correction (doi 10.1007/s12402-019-00296-6) that the citation omits.
- **Verifier notes:** The claim's own caveats are accurate. Two samples (pilot n=251, preregistered replication n=372) is a real credit, but this is cross-sectional self-report on a newly created instrument, ADHD indexed by CAARS rather than clinical diagnosis, with no behavioural measure of attention duration. NOTE A TENSION THE CLAIM GLOSSES: hyperfocus correlates with ADHD symptoms at r≈0.5 (CAARS total r(345)=0.53) but with flow at only r=0.12, and the validation authors explicitly separate the constructs — flow represents productive 'peak' experiences while 'hyperfocus may have varying outcomes.' So designing to extend hyperfocus is NOT the same as designing for flow, and nothing in this literature shows that extending an engaged bout in this population produces learning rather than time-blindness. The goal-bounded-session implication is defensible as UX judgement, not as an evidence-derived rule.

### Whether adults with ADHD show a steeper within-session performance decline (time-on-task decrement) is genuinely UNRESOLVED, and the two best adult studies disagree. Tucha et al. found adults with ADHD deteriorated significantly more than controls over 20-minute tests on alertness (RT variability), selective attention (omissions) and divided attention, but NOT on task-switching/flexibility. Fuermaier et al., testing eight vigilance instruments ranging from ~6.5 to ~33 minutes, found no differential time-on-task effect at all and concluded ADHD attention deficits 'may not depend on task duration but unfold also in short-term tasks.'

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Tucha, L., Fuermaier, A. B. M., Koerts, J., Buggenthin, R., Aschenbrenner, S., Weisbrod, M., Thome, J., Lange, K. W., & Tucha, O. (2015/2017). Sustained attention in adult ADHD: time-on-task effects of various measures of attention. Journal of Neural Transmission, 124(Suppl 1), 39–53. AND Fuermaier, A. B. M., Tucha, L., Guo, N., Mette, C., Müller, B. W., Scherbaum, N., & Tucha, O. (2022). It Takes Time: Vigilance and Sustained Attention Assessment in Adults with ADHD. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 19(9), 5216.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9102294/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** BOTH ADULT. Tucha 2015: case-control, 29 adults with ADHD (M=33.5 yrs; 8 inattentive, 21 combined, DSM-IV clinical interview by two clinicians) vs 30 controls (M=33.4); four ~20-min Vienna Test System tasks each split into four 5-min blocks. Fuermaier 2022: 31 adults with ADHD (21–64 yrs, M=37.3), NO CONTROL GROUP — compared against test-manual norms; eight tests, 6.5–33 min. Both small-n; the 2022 study's missing control group is a serious limitation for a time-on-task question.
- **Effect size:** Tucha 2015: alertness RT variability r=0.42, p=.001; selective-attention omissions r=0.44, p=.001; divided-attention RT r=0.37, p=.004 and omissions r=0.29, p=.027; flexibility null. Fuermaier 2022: null; no effect sizes reported.
- **Transfer to this app:** High relevance, low certainty. The honest takeaway is that the app must derive fatigue thresholds empirically per user rather than import a literature constant — which is fortunately what an adaptive algorithm is for.
- **Design implication:** Do not hardcode a session-length cap or a fixed break cadence from an assumed decrement — the adult evidence does not support a specific number. Instead measure it: bucket each session into ~5-minute blocks (the granularity at which Tucha found decline) and track per-block accuracy, timing SD, and omission/miss rate. Trigger a change of activity only when THAT user's own block-over-block trend degrades beyond their personal noise band. Two design notes from the pattern of results: the decrement showed up in variability and misses before it showed up in mean accuracy, so use timing standard deviation and dropped-note rate as the early-warning signal; and task-switching was spared, so a switch of activity type (new piece, new drill modality) is a better intervention than a rest break.
- **Verifier notes:** Both papers exist and every specific verifies. Tucha: 29 ADHD (M=33.5) vs 30 controls (M=33.4), DSM-IV clinical interview, 8 inattentive / 21 combined / 0 hyperactive-only; each ~20-min test 'split into 4 time blocks.' Differential decline in alertness RT variability (r=0.42), selective-attention omissions (r=0.44), divided-attention RTs (r=0.37) and omissions (r=0.29); flexibility showed no significant group difference in TOT for speed or accuracy costs. Volume/pages verified as 124(Suppl 1):39–53 per PMC5281679. Fuermaier: 31 adults, 21–64 yrs (M=37.29), NO healthy control group — 'comparisons made to test norms provided by test publishers'; ~7–33 min tests; 'no evidence for sustained attention impairments as shown by similar TOT effects in patients with ADHD compared to the respective normative groups'; conclusion quote verified verbatim. The 'unresolved' framing is the right epistemic call and the claim flags the missing control group itself. TWO CORRECTIONS TO THE DERIVED DESIGN NOTES: (a) 'the decrement showed up in variability and misses before it showed up in mean accuracy' is a loose inference — Tucha's divided-attention decline appeared in mean REACTION TIMES, a central-tendency measure, and neither paper tested block-by-block onset ordering across measures; (b) 'task-switching was spared, so switching activity type beats a rest break' is a substantial leap — Tucha found no differential TOT decline on a flexibility TEST, which says nothing about whether switching activities restores performance. The measurement-not-assumption implication (per-user ~5-min block tracking) is well grounded; the specific intervention choice is not.

### Spaced practice does NOT reliably help piano motor-skill acquisition at short lags, and practice-variability benefits do not survive consolidation. Wiseheart et al. found no spacing effect on any measure — note accuracy, timing consistency, pressure consistency, or melody reproduction — across lags of 0, 1, 5, 10 and 15 minutes, and attributed it to insufficient forgetting during the lag. Bangert et al. found variable practice conferred a transfer advantage immediately after training but the pattern REVERSED at 24-hour retention, with only the variable group showing significantly higher transfer errors.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Wiseheart, M., D'Souza, A. A., & Chae, J. (2017). Lack of spacing effects during piano learning. PLOS ONE, 12(8), e0182986. AND Bangert, M., Wiedemann, A., & Jabusch, H.-C. (2014). Effects of variability of practice in music: a pilot study on fast goal-directed movements in pianists. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 8, 598.
- **URL:** https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0182986
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** BOTH ADULT, both NEUROTYPICAL (no ADHD sample). Wiseheart 2017: N=100 university students, 94 with no prior piano training; between-subjects lag conditions 0/1/5/10/15 min; fixed 5-min retention interval; Task 1 = 17-note sequence with sheet music, Task 2 = 4–7 note melodies from five songs memorized. Bangert 2014: explicitly a PILOT, n=36 music students (M=20.5 yrs) with piano as minor, randomized to fixed (160 reps of target), variable (40 trials each of four intervals), or spaced (40 target trials with 3-min breaks); single 30-min session; 24-hr retention test.
- **Effect size:** Wiseheart: Task 1 accuracy F(4,95)=2.24, p=.07, η²=.09 (ns); timing consistency F(4,95)=0.57, p=.68, η²=.02; pressure consistency F(4,95)=0.28, p=.90, η²=.01; Task 2 F<1. Bangert: 'only partial support' for variability-of-practice; no significant primary error improvement after the single session; interaction reversed at 24 hrs.

## Reinforcement learning, reward processing, and feedback scheduling in ADHD (adult-weighted) — evidence for a piano-practice app's feedback/reward algorithm

### Ventral striatal responsiveness during reward ANTICIPATION (not delivery) is reliably hyporesponsive in ADHD — a medium effect. This is the strongest empirical support for the phasic/tonic imbalance family of models, notably Tripp & Wickens' Dopamine Transfer Deficit (DTD; Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 2008, 49(7), 691–704), which holds that the phasic dopamine response fails to TRANSFER from the reward back onto the reward-predicting cue. Consequence: a cue that merely promises a future reward does not acquire the reinforcing power to bridge the gap; the reward itself still registers normally. Note the paradox the same paper documents: in healthy samples, trait impulsivity correlates POSITIVELY with ventral striatal reward activation, so 'impulsive' and 'ADHD' are not the same neural phenotype.

Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 38, 125–134. (DTD theory: Tripp, G., & Wickens, J.R. (2008). JCPP, 49(7), 691–704.)
- **URL:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3989497/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Meta-analytic review of the fMRI reward-processing literature in ADHD (children, adolescents and adults across included studies), plus review of ventral-striatum/impulsivity studies in healthy samples. Reported effect size for VS hyporesponsiveness during anticipation: Cohen's d = 0.48–0.58 (medium).

### CONTRADICTORY / HEADLINE FINDING: in ADULTS with ADHD, IMMEDIATE feedback impaired probabilistic learning, while 3 s and 6 s DELAYED feedback produced performance indistinguishable from controls. The effect held in both the learning and the no-feedback test phase, and ADHD symptom severity correlated negatively with immediate-feedback learning specifically (r = −0.38 to −0.39). The authors' mechanism: immediate feedback forces reliance on fast striatal/phasic-dopamine procedural learning (impaired in ADHD), whereas delaying feedback shifts the load onto hippocampal declarative memory (intact). This directly inverts the standard 'ADHD needs instant feedback' design maxim for one specific kind of learning: extracting a probabilistic cue→outcome contingency.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Gabay, Y., Shahbari-Khateb, E., & Mendelsohn, A. (2018). Feedback timing modulates probabilistic learning in adults with ADHD. Scientific Reports, 8, 15524.
- **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-33551-3
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** 26 adults with ADHD vs 28 matched controls (N=54), university students, mean age ~25–26 y; formal diagnosis by paediatric neurologist plus DSM-5 screen (≥5 symptoms). Probabilistic cue–outcome learning task (Asian characters → Hebrew letters, 83%/17% contingencies), feedback at 0 s / 3 s / 6 s; 180 learning trials + 180 test trials without feedback. ### OPPOSING DIRECTION, DIFFERENT POPULATION AND TASK: in CHILDREN with ADHD, imposing a delay between stimulus and choice degraded conditional discrimination learning at 8 s and 16 s but NOT at 0 s — the classic 'steep and short delay-of-reinforcement gradient' pattern. Critically, the deficit was NOT mediated by working memory or delay aversion. Two manipulations each independently normalized ADHD performance to control level: (1) reward optimization (adding a salient €10 incentive; ADHD 72.5% → 83.6%) and (2) DIFFERENTIAL OUTCOMES — making the reward signal itself response-specific (a blue smiley for one stimulus–response pair, yellow for another) rather than a generic randomly-coloured smiley. Adding a primary (tangible) reinforcer on top of the secondary one added nothing.

### CONTRADICTS THE DOMINANT THEORIES: in the largest well-defined sample to date, children with ADHD learned more slowly IRRESPECTIVE of reinforcement schedule (needing more trials to criterion under BOTH continuous and partial reinforcement), and — against the explicit predictions of Sagvolden's Dynamic Developmental Theory and Tripp & Wickens' DTD — showed NO slower extinction. After partial reinforcement they actually made FEWER target responses during extinction than typically developing children, i.e. the Partial Reinforcement Extinction Effect did not protect them. This undercuts the popular design inference that intermittent/variable reward will make ADHD behaviour more persistent. Related contradictions on reward frequency: Aase & Sagvolden (2006, JCPP 47(5), 457–471; 56 boys, VI-2 s vs VI-20 s) found ADHD deficits under INFREQUENT reward only, supporting frequency; but Luman et al. (2009, Psychiatry Research 168(3), 222–229; 23 ADHD boys vs 30 controls vs 21 ASD) found stimulus–response learning in ADHD UNAFFECTED by either reward frequency or magnitude relative to controls. The frequency literature is genuinely unresolved.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Hulsbosch, A.-K., De Meyer, H., Beckers, T., Danckaerts, M., Van Liefferinge, D., Tripp, G., & Van der Oord, S. (2023). - **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** 93 children with ADHD vs 73 typically developing children; instrumental learning to criterion under continuous vs partial reinforcement schedules, followed by an extinction phase measuring behavioural persistence. Large, well-characterized sample; CHILDREN ONLY.

- **Design implication:** DO NOT BUILD A VARIABLE-RATIO / LOOT-BOX REWARD SCHEDULE AND EXPECT PERSISTENCE. The gambling-schedule argument ('unpredictable rewards resist extinction') is popular-press, not established for ADHD learners, and this study found the opposite direction. Two concrete rules: (1) keep the micro tier (per-correct-note particles) reliably CONTINUOUS and contingent — never randomly withhold it to manufacture uncertainty; (2) never fully withdraw reward feedback from mastered material, because faster-than-normal extinction is the risk profile here, not slower. Put the variability where SPEC §6 already puts it — in the VISUAL IDENTITY of the celebration tiers (varying the form of the reward to prevent habituation) — not in WHETHER the reward arrives. Variety of presentation, certainty of delivery.

### CONTRADICTORY, AND THE ONLY ADULT CONTINUOUS-VS-PARTIAL EVIDENCE I FOUND: continuous (100%) reward produced POORER vigilance performance in adults with attentional deficits than partial (30%) reward — the decrement appeared early in the reward phase and persisted through extinction. Frustration (measured objectively as key-press force) did not differ between groups across schedules. The authors interpret this via arousal: a fully predictable reward stops carrying information and stops driving arousal, so performance decays. Adults with attentional deficits were also slower and more variable in RT than controls throughout, mirroring the child literature.

## Adult ADHD: self-directed practice, task initiation, sustained attention over time-on-task, gamification, goal setting, and adherence — evidence review for a solo piano-practice app

### Adults with ADHD showed a steeper decline in attention performance across four 20-minute tasks (analysed in 5-minute blocks) than controls, with medium effect sizes on alertness variability (r=0.42), selective-attention omissions (r=0.44) and divided-attention RT (r=0.37) and omissions (r=0.29). Deterioration was measurable within the first 5-10 minutes and continued through 20 minutes.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Tucha L, Fuermaier ABM, Koerts J, Buggenthin R, Aschenbrenner S, Weisbrod M, Thome J, Lange KW, Tucha O (2015/2017). Sustained attention in adult ADHD: time-on-task effects of various measures of attention. Journal of Neural Transmission, 124(Suppl 1), 39-53.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5281679/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** 29 adults with ADHD vs 30 healthy controls; four 20-min computerised attention tasks split into 5-min blocks; small-n case-control, adults only (good age match to target user).
- **Effect size:** Medium time-on-task group differences, r = 0.29-0.44
- **Transfer to this app:** Direct on population (adults with ADHD) but indirect on task: these are lab CPT-style tasks, not motivating sensorimotor music tasks. Piano practice with continuous audio-visual feedback is far more stimulating than a CPT, so the real decay point is likely later. Treat 5-8 min as a starting prior to be replaced by measured per-user data, not as a fact about piano.
- **Design implication:** Set the default drill block at roughly 5-8 minutes of continuous demand, not 20. Structure a session as a chain of short blocks with a state change between them rather than one long continuous highway run. Log per-block accuracy/timing so the app can detect this user's own decay curve instead of assuming a population value.
- **Verifier notes:** Both papers exist and every specific verifies. Tucha: 29 ADHD (M=33.5) vs 30 controls (M=33.4), DSM-IV clinical interview, 8 inattentive / 21 combined / 0 hyperactive-only; each ~20-min test 'split into 4 time blocks.' Volume/pages verified as 124(Suppl 1):39–53 per PMC5281679. The 'unresolved' framing is the right epistemic call and the claim flags the missing control group itself. TWO CORRECTIONS TO THE DERIVED DESIGN NOTES: (a) 'the decrement showed up in variability and misses before it showed up in mean accuracy' is a loose inference — Tucha's divided-attention decline appeared in mean REACTION TIMES, a central-tendency measure, and neither paper tested block-by-block onset ordering across measures; (b) 'task-switching was spared, so switching activity type beats a rest break' is a substantial leap — Tucha found no differential TOT decline on a flexibility TEST, which says nothing about whether switching activities restores performance. The measurement-not-assumption implication (per-user ~5-min block tracking) is well grounded; the specific intervention choice is not.

### A larger and more thorough adult replication found NO consistent sustained-attention (time-on-task) deficit: across eight vigilance tests, about half the ADHD patients deteriorated more over time than norms and half deteriorated less. Overall attention performance was 1-3 SD below normative means from the start. The authors concluded attention deficits in adult ADHD 'may not depend on task duration'.

- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9102294/

- **Effect size:** No systematic TOT group effect; baseline decrements ~1-3 SD below norms

Make drill length adaptive and data-driven: end a block on a measured performance criterion (e.g. rolling-window accuracy or timing-variability crossing a per-user threshold) rather than on a fixed clock. Keep the clock only as a hard outer bound for session boundaries.

### Intra-individual reaction-time variability, not mean speed, is the robust cognitive signature of ADHD: adults with ADHD showed g=0.46 greater RT variability than typically developing controls (children/adolescents g=0.76). After controlling for variability, mean RT differences disappeared; large variability deficits remained after controlling for mean RT.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · STRONG
- **Citation:** Kofler MJ, Rapport MD, Sarver DE, Raiker JS, Orban SA, Friedman LM, Kolomeyer EG (2013). Reaction time variability in ADHD: A meta-analytic review of 319 studies. Clinical Psychology Review, 33(6), 795-811.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23872284/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Meta-analysis of 319 studies; separate adult stratum reported; includes clinical control comparisons (variability is not ADHD-specific vs other clinical groups).
- **Effect size:** g = 0.46 (adults); stimulant treatment attenuated variability, g = -0.74
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong transfer. The construct (moment-to-moment motor-response variability) is close to what a MIDI timing pipeline already measures natively, and the app has ground truth from MusicXML. This is probably the single most actionable finding for the scoring algorithm.
- **Design implication:** Make timing-error variability (SD or IQR of note-onset deviation within a passage) a first-class displayed metric, alongside accuracy. Score and gate progression on consistency, not on best-attempt or mean. A run of 20 notes with tight variance is a better 'mastered' signal than a lower mean error with occasional 200ms lapses. Also: occasional catastrophic lapses are expected and should not be treated as regression — use trimmed/robust statistics in the mastery estimator so one lapse does not reset a passage.
- **Verifier notes:** Verified verbatim against the paper. Abstract: 'children/adolescents (Hedges' g = 0.76) and adults (g = 0.46) with ADHD demonstrated greater RT variability relative to TD groups... Individuals with ADHD did not evince slower processing speed (mean RT) after accounting for RT variability, whereas large magnitude RT variability deficits remained after accounting for mean RT.' Adult stratum: g=0.46, 95% CI 0.31-0.61. k=283 for the ADHD-vs-TD tier. Three caveats the claim does not carry, all of which MATTER for the implication: (a) the 'mean RT disappears' result rests on only EIGHT studies (ADHD n=447, TD n=457; g=-0.19, 95% CI -0.46 to 0.08, ns) and the authors explicitly say 'results should therefore be interpreted with caution' — this is the weakest link in an otherwise strong meta-analysis; (b) g=0.46 in adults corresponds to ~70% population overlap, i.e. most individual adults with ADHD fall inside the typical range, so variability is a group-level signature, not an individual diagnostic; (c) adults with ADHD were INDISTINGUISHABLE from clinical control groups (g=-0.06, ns) — variability is not ADHD-specific. None of this undermines the IMPLICATION (score consistency, use robust/trimmed statistics, don't treat one lapse as regression), which is sound engineering advice and the strongest-grounded implication in the set.

### Vigilance decrement over a 50-minute task was completely eliminated by two brief, rare switches to an unrelated secondary task — the 'switch' group's performance at minute 50 matched minute 1, whereas the no-break control declined significantly. The proposed mechanism is that briefly deactivating and reactivating the task goal prevents goal habituation.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Ariga A, Lleras A (2011). Brief and rare mental 'breaks' keep you focused: Deactivation and reactivation of task goals preempt vigilance decrements. Cognition, 118(3), 439-443.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027710002994
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** n=84 undergraduates, 4-group between-subjects, 50-minute sustained-response task. Neurotypical sample — NOT an ADHD study.
- **Effect size:** Decrement fully abolished in switch condition vs significant decline in control
- **Transfer to this app:** Population transfer is the weak link — neurotypical undergraduates, single study, and the finding has a mixed replication record in the broader vigilance literature (some studies find rest breaks help, some find task interruptions help more). Use it as a design hypothesis worth A/B-ing against a plain rest screen, not as settled science. It does fit the ADHD 'optimal stimulation / cognitive-energetic state regulation' theories (Zentall & Zentall 1983; Sergeant 2005), which predict ADHD performance benefits from added task stimulation — but those theories were developed in children.
- **Design implication:** Between drill blocks, insert a short, genuinely different micro-task rather than an idle rest screen — e.g. a 20-second ear-training prompt, a tempo-matching tap, or a sight-read-one-bar interstitial. Critically, 'rare and brief' was the effective dose (two switches in 50 min), so do not fragment every 90 seconds. Also note: a passive countdown/rest screen is not what was tested; the effective manipulation was an active goal switch.
- **Verifier correction:** Two errors, one factual and one substantive. (1) The task was 40 MINUTES, not 50 — four continuous 10-min blocks of 300 trials, 1200 trials total. So 'performance at minute 50 matched minute 1' and 'two switches in 50 min' should be minute 40 and 'two switches in 40 min'. (2) 'Performance ... matched minute 1' is true only for ACCURACY. Target-detection sensitivity (A') in the Switch group did not change across blocks (F(3,240)=0.89, ns) while Control, No-switch and Digit-ignored all declined (Fs(3,240)>4.62, p<.005). But for REACTION TIMES there was a significant main effect of Block (F(3,240)=23.48, p<.001) with NO Condition x Block interaction (F(9,240)=0.93, ns) — every group including Switch slowed down over the 40 minutes. Accurate version: 'Over a 40-minute vigilance task, two brief switches to an unrelated memory probe fully preserved detection SENSITIVITY (no decrement, vs significant decline in all three other groups), although response times slowed equivalently over time in every condition including the switch group.'
- **Verifier notes:** Design verified: 84 students (37 male) in 4 between-subjects groups (Control n=21, No-switch n=24, Switch n=23, Digit-ignored n=16 = 84). Digits appeared after trials 600, 900 and 1200; the 1200th is the final trial, so there were exactly TWO mid-task switches — the paper itself says 'two power breaks'. The claim's own caveats are all correct and important: neurotypical undergraduates, NOT an ADHD study, and the effective manipulation was an ACTIVE goal switch, not a passive rest (the Digit-ignored group saw identical stimuli and still declined, which is the paper's cleanest control and directly supports the claim's 'a countdown/rest screen is not what was tested'). Strength MODERATE: single well-designed and widely cited study, n=84, but never replicated in ADHD and the paper itself concedes the data are also consistent with a Yerkes-Dodson arousal account. The IMPLICATION (rare, brief, genuinely different interstitial rather than an idle screen; do not fragment every 90 seconds) tracks the paper well once the 40-min figure is fixed — but note the transfer from a line-detection vigil to piano practice is an inference, not a finding.

### Counterintuitively, adults with ADHD were significantly WORSE than controls at probabilistic learning with immediate (0s) feedback (F(1,47)=6.58, p<0.01) but performed equivalently to controls with 3s or 6s delayed feedback. ADHD symptom severity correlated negatively with learning from immediate feedback (r = -0.38 to -0.39) and not at all with learning from delayed feedback.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Gabay Y, Shahbari-Khateb E, Mendelsohn A (2018). Feedback timing modulates probabilistic learning in adults with ADHD. Scientific Reports, 8, 15524.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195519/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** 54 university students (26 ADHD, 28 control), mean age ~25-26; within-subject feedback-delay manipulation (0s / 3s / 6s), 60 trials per condition. Single study, small n, one task paradigm.
- **Effect size:** Group x timing interaction p<0.01; symptom-severity r ≈ -0.39 for immediate only
- **Transfer to this app:** Adults with ADHD — good population match. Task transfer is weak: probabilistic category learning (declarative/striatal) is not motor sequence learning, and the mechanism proposed (delay shifts learning away from an impaired striatal system) may not apply to piano. ### In general learning contexts, gamification produced small-to-moderate effects: cognitive learning outcomes g=0.49 (k=19, N=1686), motivational g=0.36 (k=16, N=2246), behavioural g=0.25 (k=9, N=951). - **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9962130/
- **Peer reviewed:** True

## Retrieval practice, spacing, contextual interference, and motor-skill acquisition — applied to keyboard-geography recall and piano motor-sequence drills (mastery criteria + drill scheduling)

### Retrieval practice (being tested) produces substantially better long-term retention than restudying the same material for the same time. Pooled g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58] across 159 effect-size comparisons of retrieval vs. restudy. The effect was LARGER when the practiced material was more complex, when retrieval was more effortful (free/cued recall > recognition), and when corrective feedback followed the retrieval attempt. Support for the 'semantic elaboration' explanation was weak, so the mechanism is still open.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · STRONG
- **Citation:** Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. Psychological Bulletin, 140(6), 1432–1463. doi:10.1037/a0037559
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Meta-analysis, 159 effect sizes from published testing-effect experiments; overwhelmingly adult (university-student) samples learning verbal/declarative material (word pairs, prose, facts). Not motor tasks.
- **Effect size:** g = 0.50 [0.42, 0.58]; moderated upward by material complexity, retrieval effort, and presence of feedback
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong and direct for the keyboard-geography half of the app: adult samples, declarative cue→response material, which is structurally what note-name→key-location is. Does NOT license claims about motor-sequence drills — no motor tasks were in this meta-analysis.
- **Design implication:** Keyboard-geography drills must require the user to PRODUCE the key from a cue (show note name / staff position → user presses the key), never to recognise or follow a highlighted key. A highlighted-key 'follow the light' mode is a restudy condition and should not count toward mastery. Feedback (correct key flashed after the attempt) should be delivered after every trial, not before. Because effortful recall beats recognition, the drill should not narrow the answer space (no 'which of these 3 keys' variants).
- **Verifier correction:** One sub-clause is wrong. Rowland's initial-test-type moderator (Table 1) is: cued recall g = 0.61 [0.52, 0.69], k = 104; FREE recall g = 0.29 [0.07, 0.52], k = 36; recognition g = 0.29 [0.10, 0.47], k = 19. Free recall was identical to recognition, not better. The correct statement is 'CUED recall > free recall = recognition.' Also, 'material complexity' was not a directly coded moderator; the claim is an inference from the stimulus-type moderator (prose 0.58, paired associates 0.59, single words 0.39, other 0.27).
- **Verifier notes:** Article verified via Crossref and full text (PDF). Headline figures are verbatim: g = 0.50 (CI [0.42, 0.58]), Z = 12.38, p < .001, 159 effect sizes. Feedback moderator verbatim: no feedback g = 0.39 (k = 107) vs feedback g = 0.73 (k = 52), QB = 18.72, p < .01. Retention interval: <1 day 0.41, >=1 day 0.69. Abstract confirms 'Limited support was found for existing theoretical accounts attributing the testing effect to enhanced semantic elaboration.' Unreported caveats: high heterogeneity (tau^2 = 0.21, Q = 1009.42); published g = 0.58 vs unpublished g = 0.25 (a publication-bias signal); and the 'retrievability' moderator shows no-feedback studies with <=50% initial recall success had g = 0.03 [-0.21, 0.27] — i.e. the effect vanishes when retrieval mostly fails without feedback. That last point strengthens the app implication about always giving feedback. The design implication (produce, don't recognise; don't narrow the answer space) survives the correction, since cued recall 0.61 > recognition 0.29 is the relevant contrast for a cue->key drill.

### The testing effect extends to procedural/motor skill learning, not just facts. Replacing 30 min of a 4-hour resuscitation skills course with 30 min of testing (vs. 4 h of pure practice) raised later skill scores from 73.3% (95% CI 70.5–76.1) to 82.8% (95% CI 79.4–86.2), p < 0.001, effect size 0.93 — i.e. time spent being tested beat an equal amount of time spent practising.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Kromann, C. B., Jensen, M. L., & Ringsted, C. (2009). The effect of testing on skills learning. Medical Education, 43(1), 21–27. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03245.x
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19140995/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Prospective, randomised, single-blind, post-test-only trial; n = 140 medical students (81 analysed across arms), simulation-based in-hospital resuscitation skills. Adults, real motor/procedural task, but a multi-step checklist-scoreable procedure rather than a continuous fine-motor sequence.
- **Effect size:** d = 0.93 (9.5 percentage-point difference in skill score)
- **Transfer to this app:** Moderate. Adult, procedural, and time-equated — the strongest available evidence that retrieval helps skills. But confounded: the 'test' condition also meant one uninterrupted whole-task performance, which differs from piano micro-drills. Treat as supportive, not conclusive, for motor sequences.
- **Design implication:** Budget explicit, scored, no-help 'test' reps inside every session rather than spending all session time on assisted practice. Concretely: after N assisted reps of a passage, force at least one unassisted, scored full-speed attempt — and let that scored attempt (not the assisted reps) be the only thing that advances the item's mastery state. Do not treat testing as assessment overhead stolen from practice time; the evidence says it IS practice time, better spent.
- **Verifier notes:** Citation exact (JRME 56(4):310–321, doi 10.1177/0022429408328851). Null verified: 'no significant relationship between the rankings of pianists' retention test performances and ... practice time, number of total practice trials, and number of complete practice trials.' Strategy characterization verified: top-ranked pianists were better able to identify and pinpoint the source of errors, rehearse and correct them, vary the tempo of problem segments systematically, and repeat practice targets until errors were fixed. Design: 17 graduate/advanced-undergraduate piano majors, three-measure Shostakovich concerto passage, retention test the FOLLOWING day — the claim's design note omits the overnight interval, which actually strengthens the design. STRENGTH WEAK and the claim's own caveats are honest. This is observational and correlational at n=17, so the null on practice time is an UNDERPOWERED null — with 17 participants a genuine moderate correlation would likely be missed. Read it as 'quantity did not predict retention among 17 elite pianists on one passage,' not as 'quantity does not matter.' The population is also the inverse of the target user: conservatory-level pianists already possess the self-regulation the app must supply. The behaviours are confounded with skill (better pianists both practise better and play better), and nothing here shows that imposing the behaviours on a novice produces the outcome. The derived implications are nonetheless low-risk and consistent with the broader deliberate-practice literature.

### For repeated retrieval, the TOTAL (absolute) elapsed spacing between tests is what drives long-term retention; the SHAPE of the schedule (expanding vs. uniform vs. contracting) is nearly irrelevant once retrieval success is equated. Widely spaced repeated retrieval produced a ~200% improvement in long-term retention over massed repeated retrieval. Critically, items that were recalled once and then dropped from further practice showed markedly worse long-term retention than items retrieved three times.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Karpicke, J. D., & Bauernschmidt, A. (2011). Spaced retrieval: Absolute spacing enhances learning regardless of relative spacing. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 37(5), 1250–1257. doi:10.1037/a0023436
- **URL:** https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Bauernschmidt_JEPLMC.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Adult university students; foreign-language word pairs; conditions included study-once, criterion-of-one-correct-then-dropped, massed repeated retrieval, and several spacing schedules with retrieval success experimentally equated across schedules.
- **Effect size:** ~200% (3x) advantage of long absolute spacing over zero spacing between repeated tests
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong for keyboard geography. This is the finding that most cheaply de-risks the scheduler: it says the SM-2/FSRS-style interval-curve sophistication is largely wasted effort even within declarative memory, so a crude spacing rule is defensible. Untested for motor sequences.
- **Design implication:** Two hard rules for the scheduler. (1) Never retire a keyboard-geography item after a single correct response — 'got it once, done' is the single worst schedule tested. Require multiple spaced successes. (2) Do not invest engineering effort in tuning the interval curve's shape (expanding multipliers, ease factors); invest it in guaranteeing total elapsed time between the repetitions of an item and in making sure repetitions land in different sessions. A simple 'each item must be hit K times with total elapsed spacing ≥ T' rule captures most of the available benefit.
- **Verifier correction:** Two errors, one factual and one substantive. (1) The task was 40 MINUTES, not 50 — four continuous 10-min blocks of 300 trials, 1200 trials total. So 'performance at minute 50 matched minute 1' and 'two switches in 50 min' should be minute 40 and 'two switches in 40 min'. (2) 'Performance ... matched minute 1' is true only for ACCURACY. Target-detection sensitivity (A') in the Switch group did not change across blocks (F(3,240)=0.89, ns) while Control, No-switch and Digit-ignored all declined (Fs(3,240)>4.62, p<.005). Digits appeared after trials 600, 900 and 1200; the 1200th is the final trial, so there were exactly TWO mid-task switches — the paper itself says 'two power breaks'. The claim's own caveats are all correct and important: neurotypical undergraduates, NOT an ADHD study, and the effective manipulation was an ACTIVE goal switch, not a passive rest (the Digit-ignored group saw identical stimuli and still declined, which is the paper's cleanest control and directly supports the claim's 'a countdown/rest screen is not what was tested'). The IMPLICATION (rare, brief, genuinely different interstitial rather than an idle screen; do not fragment every 90 seconds) tracks the paper well once the 40-min figure is fixed — but note the transfer from a line-detection vigil to piano practice is an inference, not a finding.

### Expanding retrieval schedules beat equally spaced ones on a short-delay test but LOSE on a delayed test — a direct performance/learning reversal. Expanding: 71% vs. equal 62% at a 10-min delay (d = 0.30); reversed to equal 45% vs. expanding 33% at 2 days (d = 0.50). Replicated with feedback (90% vs. 87% at 10 min; equal 60% vs. expanding 49% at 2 days, d = 0.41). The active ingredient was DELAYING THE FIRST retrieval attempt: a single test given after a lag beat an immediate test at 2 days (30% vs. 22%, d = 0.41) despite losing at 10 min. Expanding the later intervals did not increase retrieval difficulty as theory assumed.

- **Peer reviewed:** True

### Criterion-based mastery has a measured optimum: practise recalling an item to a criterion of 3 CORRECT RECALLS in the initial session, then relearn it to 1 correct recall in 3 widely spaced later sessions. Higher initial criteria (4+) bought little extra; a criterion of 1 was clearly insufficient. Raising the criterion level strengthens the underlying association itself (better forward cued recall, backward cued recall, AND associative recognition), not merely surface familiarity.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2011). Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough? Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 140(3), 283–302. doi:10.1037/a0023956 — together with Vaughn, K. E., & Rawson, K. A. (2011). Diagnosing criterion-level effects on memory: What aspects of memory are enhanced by repeated retrieval? Psychological Science, 22(9), 1127–1131. doi:10.1177/0956797611417724
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707189/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Rawson & Dunlosky: 3 experiments, n = 533 adult students, conceptual/definitional material, criterion of 1–4 correct recalls crossed with 1–5 spaced relearning sessions. Vaughn & Rawson: adults, Lithuanian–English word pairs, criterion 1–5, with forward/backward cued recall and associative recognition as outcomes.
- **Effect size:** Prescriptive optimum reported as criterion of 3 correct recalls + 3 spaced relearning sessions; efficiency (retention per unit study time) peaked there rather than at the highest criterion
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong and directly implementable for keyboard geography (adults, cue→response, self-paced). Explicitly NOT validated for motor sequences — do not reuse the same '3 correct' counter for a passage-playing item without separate justification.
- **Design implication:** Concrete mastery rule for keyboard-geography items: '3 correct recalls in the first session (non-consecutive, spaced by other items), then 1 correct recall in each of 3 later sessions.' Then stop drilling it. This is a defensible, literature-anchored replacement for an arbitrary accuracy threshold like '90% over last 20 trials', and it is cheap to store (a small integer per item + last-session stamp). Note it is 'N correct', NOT 'N correct in a row' — the studies did not require consecutiveness, and requiring it would create an unbounded, demoralising tail.
- **Verifier notes:** Both articles verified (Crossref + PubMed). Rawson & Dunlosky: 533 students, >100,000 hand-scored recall responses, conceptual material, durability measured 1-4 months later; the recommendation is verbatim the claim's — practise to three correct recalls initially, then relearn three times with widely spaced sessions. The mechanism they report is SUBADDITIVITY (initial-criterion benefits shrink as relearning sessions are added), which is what makes '3 then 3' the efficiency optimum rather than a durability maximum — worth keeping in the app's reasoning, because it means the rule is a cost-benefit answer, not 'more is not better.' Vaughn & Rawson verified: Lithuanian-English pairs, criterion 1-5, 2-day (Exp 1) and 1-week (Exp 2) delays; raising criterion enhanced forward and backward cued recall and associative recognition, AND free recall/recognition of targets and of cues separately — i.e. it strengthened cue memory, target memory, and the association, which is slightly broader than the claim states. Two things I could NOT verify from the available abstracts (both closed access): (a) the specific 'criterion 4+ bought little extra / criterion 1 clearly insufficient' contrast — consistent with subadditivity and the recommendation, but the exact pattern is unconfirmed; (b) the claim's confident assertion that the criterion was 'N correct, NOT N correct in a row' and that 'the studies did not require consecutiveness.' Rawson & Dunlosky's procedure interleaved items with restudy between attempts, so non-consecutive is the likely reading, but do not present this as a verified fact. Strength MODERATE: two studies from one lab, verbal/conceptual material only, zero motor content.

### Overlearning — continuing to drill within a session after the mastery criterion is reached — is an inefficient use of time for durable retention. In one experiment, 9 practice problems vs. 3 in a single session (the extra 6 being pure overlearning) had NO effect on test scores at either 1 or 4 weeks, while redistributing the SAME 10 problems across two sessions instead of one nearly DOUBLED 4-week performance. In another study (n = 218 adults), overlearners beat low learners at 1 week but the gap shrank dramatically thereafter. Contradicting evidence: a meta-analysis of overlearning found a moderate overall benefit and, notably, that participants who overlearned PHYSICAL skills held or continued improving over the retention interval while cognitive-material overlearners declined.

Effect of overlearning on retention. Journal of Applied Psychology, 77(5), 615–622.
- **URL:** http://uweb.cas.usf.edu/~drohrer/pdfs/Rohrer&Taylor2006ACP.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Rohrer & Taylor 2006: 216 college students, mathematical procedure, 1- vs 4-week retention. ### Response LATENCY, not just accuracy, indexes memory strength and predicts future forgetting: among items that were correctly recalled, the faster the correct recall, the more likely the item was still recalled at a later test — holding at 5 min, 30 min, and 24 h delays, and visible within individual subjects. Separately, automaticity has a specific signature distinguishable from mere speed-up: automatization shows the COEFFICIENT OF VARIATION of response time (SD/mean RT) falling, whereas simple speed-up reduces mean RT and SD proportionally, leaving CV unchanged.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Madigan, S., Neuse, J., & Roeber, U. (2000). Retrieval latency and "at-risk" memories. Memory & Cognition, 28(4), 523–528. doi:10.3758/BF03201242 — and Segalowitz, N. S., & Segalowitz, S. J. (1993). Skilled performance, practice, and the differentiation of speed-up from automatization effects: Evidence from second language word recognition. Applied Psycholinguistics, 14(3), 369–385.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10946535/
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Madigan et al.: three experiments, adults, five lists of 10 paired associates, single study+test trial with spoken recall latencies, retested at 5 min / 30 min / 24 h. Segalowitz & Segalowitz: adults, L2 lexical-decision RT across practice; CV computed as SD/mean RT. Both declarative/cognitive, neither motor. Note Madigan et al. contrast with earlier semantic-memory work where faster retrievals were retained WORSE, so latency's meaning is task-dependent.
- **Effect size:** Not reported as a standardised effect size; reported as a systematic monotonic relation between initial correct-recall latency and probability of recall at the second test, replicated across three retention intervals and present in individual-subject data
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong for keyboard geography (adult, cue→response, latency-measurable). Caveat: latency thresholds must be per-user and per-item-difficulty, since raw RT confounds skill with hand position and reach distance on a piano.
- **Design implication:** Make latency a first-class mastery criterion, not a cosmetic stat. A keyboard-geography item should not count as mastered on accuracy alone: gate on (correct) AND (latency below a per-user threshold). Store per-item rolling mean AND SD of response latency and compute CV = SD/mean; treat falling CV as the automaticity signal and a stable-but-high CV as 'fast sometimes, still searching' — i.e. still at risk, keep drilling. Practically: a correct-but-slow response should schedule the item sooner, not mark it done. This app is unusually well placed to do this — it already has sub-15ms keypress-to-response latency and MIDI timestamps on the delivery thread, so latency and CV are essentially free to compute in the existing hot path.
- **Verifier correction:** The latency half is accurate; the CV half is presented as an established signature when it has been directly tested and NOT supported. Hulstijn, Van Gelderen & Schoonen (2009), 'Automatization in second language acquisition: What does the coefficient of variation tell us?', Applied Psycholinguistics 30, doi:10.1017/s0142716409990014, states the Segalowitz distinction exactly as the claim does and then reports two studies against it — a 2-year longitudinal study with four RT tasks in L2 and L1 (N > 200) and a training study (N = 41): 'Convincing evidence for automatization was not found in either study,' the core problem being that 'gains in knowledge itself and gains in processing it cannot be adequately disentangled in the RT tasks currently used, characterized by a speed-accuracy trade-off.' So falling CV is a hypothesis with a well-powered failed test against it, not a validated automaticity metric. Reframe as: CV is cheap to compute and worth LOGGING as an exploratory signal, but do not gate mastery on it or present it as literature-backed.
- **Verifier notes:** Madigan et al. verified verbatim via EuropePMC: 'In three experiments, subjects were given a single study and test trial on each of five lists of 10 paired associates. Spoken recall latencies were measured... the faster the initial correct recall of a pair, the more likely a pair was to be recalled at the second test. This effect occurred at retention intervals of 5 min, 30 min, and 24 h and was present in the data for individual subjects.' The claim also correctly and creditably flags the contrast the paper itself draws with Benjamin, Bjork & Schwartz (1998), where faster SEMANTIC-memory retrievals were retained WORSE — so latency's sign is genuinely task-dependent, and a piano app's cue->key items are closer to the episodic paired-associate case than the semantic case. Segalowitz & Segalowitz (1993) verified via Crossref (Applied Psycholinguistics 14(3), 369-385) and the CV logic is stated correctly. Strength WEAK overall: the latency finding alone would be MODERATE (three experiments, one paper, one lab, declarative material, spoken recall latencies rather than keypress latencies, and a documented opposite result in a neighbouring paradigm); the CV component is contested. The practical recommendation 'a correct-but-slow response should schedule the item sooner' is still reasonable and cheap, but it should be justified as a defensible heuristic the app can validate against this one user's own cold scores, not as an established finding.

### Motor sequence memory improves WITHOUT practice, across sleep — retention functions for motor skills are non-monotonic in a way declarative forgetting curves are not. A night of sleep produced ~20% speed gain on a finger-tapping sequence with no accuracy loss, while an equivalent 12 h awake produced no significant benefit. Gains scaled with sequence complexity: 17.7% (5-element unimanual), 17.5% (5-element bimanual), 20.2% (9-element unimanual), 28.9% (9-element bimanual), alongside 29.9–47.8% reductions in error rate. Most strikingly, the individual transitions that were SLOWEST at end of training improved 17.8% overnight while the fastest transitions improved a non-significant 1.4%. Contradicting the shorter-timescale version of this idea: apparent within-session 'micro-offline gains' across 10-second rests have been shown to be transient performance effects (partly motor preplanning) that vanish seconds after training and also occur for unlearnable random sequences.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Walker, M. P., Brakefield, T., Morgan, A., Hobson, J. A., & Stickgold, R. (2002). Practice with sleep makes perfect: Sleep-dependent motor skill learning. Neuron, 35(1), 205–211 — Kuriyama, K., Stickgold, R., & Walker, M. P. (2004). Sleep-dependent learning and motor-skill complexity. Learning & Memory, 11(6), 705–713 — contradicting the micro-timescale claim: Das, A., Karagiorgis, A., Diedrichsen, J., Stenner, M.-P., & Azañón, E. (2025). Micro-offline gains do not reflect offline learning during early motor skill acquisition in humans. PNAS, 122. doi:10.1073/pnas.2509233122 (cf. Bönstrup et al., 2019, Current Biology, 29(8), 1346–1351)
- **URL:** https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/11/6/705.full
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Kuriyama et al.: n = 57 healthy right-handed adults (18–31), between-subjects, four sequence conditions, trained 1 PM Day 1 and retested 1 PM Day 2 after a night's sleep. Walker et al.: adults, sequential finger-tapping, trained morning or evening and retested at 12-h wake vs. sleep intervals. These are finger-tapping sequences on a keyboard, not musical performance; short (5–9 element) sequences; young adults.
- **Effect size:** ~20% overnight speed gain with no accuracy cost; 17.5–28.9% by condition, scaling with complexity; error rate down 29.9–47.8%; hardest transitions +17.8% vs. easiest +1.4% (ns)
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong on population (young/mid adults) and reasonably close on task (keyboard finger sequences), which makes this the best-supported motor finding for a piano app. Weaker on materials: 5–9 element sequences at maximum speed are not musical passages with expressive timing, and the outcome measure was raw speed/error, not musicality. The SM-2/FSRS incompatibility argument is my inference from these data, not a claim any of these papers tests directly — no peer-reviewed study validates a declarative spaced-repetition scheduler on motor sequence retention.
- **Design implication:** Three implications. (1) Never evaluate a motor-sequence item's mastery in the session that introduced it — the score will be measured before the largest single chunk of improvement has happened. Gate motor mastery on a cold FIRST attempt in a later session, after at least one night's sleep. (2) A day-scale scheduler for motor items should EXPECT improvement across a gap and must not interpret a same-or-better cold score as 'no learning happened, drill harder'. (3) This is the load-bearing reason NOT to port SM-2/FSRS to motor items: those algorithms are built on a monotonically decaying probability-of-recall and choose intervals to catch an item before it decays. Motor sequences can be BETTER after a gap than at the end of the last session, so the model's core state variable is mis-specified for them. Use SRS-style scheduling for keyboard-geography items only, and schedule motor items on measured cold performance (speed, error rate, timing evenness) instead. Corollary from the complexity scaling: harder/longer sequences and specifically the slowest transitions benefit MOST overnight, so the app should identify the slowest transitions within a passage and re-test them cold the next day rather than grinding them the same evening. And per Das et al., do not build features that advertise 10-second rest breaks as producing consolidation — that specific claim has been refuted.
- **Verifier correction:** Two corrections, the second decisive. (1) 'Gains scaled with sequence complexity' overstates a step function as a gradient: Kuriyama's own abstract says 'subjects show SIMILAR overnight improvements in speed whether learning a five-element unimanual sequence (17.7%), a nine-element unimanual sequence (20.2%), or a five-element bimanual sequence (17.5%), but show markedly increased overnight improvement (28.9%) with a nine-element bimanual sequence.' Only the hardest of four conditions stood out, from a between-subjects design with n = 14-15 per group. (2) The claim cites the 2025 refutation of the 10-SECOND micro-offline version while omitting the meta-analytic refutation of the OVERNIGHT version it actually rests on. Thus, the literature speaks against, rather than for, the enhancement hypothesis... We conclude that sleep does not enhance motor learning.' Rickard, Pan & Gupta (2022), JEP:LMC 48, 1787-1796, doi:10.1037/xlm0001090, then applied PET/PEESE to the same 88 effect sizes and found severe publication bias, with a bias-corrected 'estimated postsleep gain [that] was negative, suggesting forgetting rather than facilitation, and it was statistically indistinguishable from the estimated postwake gain.' The honest version: absolute overnight ENHANCEMENT of motor sequence performance is not established; at most sleep may stabilise rather than improve, and even that 'cannot be conclusively determined based on the literature to date.'
- **Verifier notes:** Every primary number the claim cites is exact — I verified them against the Kuriyama full text (learnmem.cshlp.org PDF) and the Walker abstract. Kuriyama verbatim: 'A total of 57 healthy right-handed subjects between the ages of 18 and 31 [mean 22.5 +/- 1.22 (SD); 40 females]', all groups 'trained at 1 PM on Day 1, and following one night of sleep, were retested at 1 PM on Day 2'; group ns 15/14/14/14; overnight error-rate reductions 47.8% (Group 1) down to 29.9% (Group 4); 'individual transitions within the motor-sequence pattern that appeared most difficult at the end of training showed a significant 17.8% increase in speed overnight, whereas those transitions that were performed most rapidly at the end of training showed only a non-significant 1.4% improvement.' Das et al. 2025 verified in full and matches the claim's summary precisely, including that MOGs persisted for random non-repeating sequences and were diminished when preplanning was blocked. So this is not a fabrication problem — it is an evidence-strength problem, and it matters because this claim is doing the most architectural work in the set (it is the stated reason not to port SM-2/FSRS to motor items). The narrower, better-supported version of the implication survives the correction and is worth keeping: do not evaluate a motor item's mastery in the session that introduced it, and gate on a cold next-session first attempt — that follows from the acquisition/retention dissociation (claims 4, 7) and from reactive-inhibition/fatigue effects alone, without needing sleep to ENHANCE anything. What does not survive is 'expect improvement across the gap' as a modelling assumption and the strong 'probability-of-recall is mis-specified for motor items' argument.

### Fixed/conventional spacing schedules (uniform, expanding, contracting, or drop-after-correct) are all confounded by item- and learner-level differences in difficulty, and are outperformed by scheduling that quantitatively tracks each item's current recall probability and selects the item with the greatest expected gain per unit of time. Simulations plus experiment showed up to 40% MORE items recalled under model-based adaptive scheduling than under conventional schedules. The paper also notes that response times during practice carry scheduling-relevant information beyond correct/incorrect: wider spacing yielded better learning but significantly longer practice RTs.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Eglington, L. G., & Pavlik, P. I., Jr. (2020). Optimizing practice scheduling requires quantitative tracking of individual item performance. npj Science of Learning, 5, 15. doi:10.1038/s41539-020-00074-4
- **URL:** https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED608719.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Simulation study plus human experiments with adults learning declarative concepts and rule application; compares conventional schedules against a computational model of spacing combined with microeconomic gain-per-time item selection. Declarative material only; no motor tasks.
- **Effect size:** Up to 40% more items recalled vs. conventional schedules
- **Transfer to this app:** Strong for the keyboard-geography scheduler (adult, declarative, self-paced, item-level). Transfer to motor items is unestablished, and the 40% figure comes from vocabulary-style learning — do not expect it on passages. Its main value here is architectural: it justifies a gain-per-time selection policy over an interval-curve algorithm, which happens to also be the simpler thing to build.
- **Design implication:** Structure the scheduler as 'pick the item with the highest expected retention gain per second of drill time', with per-item state = (successes at criterion, last-seen timestamp, rolling mean latency, rolling latency SD). This is a better fit for a single-user local-SQLite app than a ported SM-2/FSRS ease-factor scheme: it needs no population-fitted parameters, it naturally consumes the latency data the MIDI path already produces, and it degrades gracefully with sparse history. Store the raw per-trial (item, correct, latency, timestamp) log in Room so the selection policy can be changed later without losing history — the policy will need tuning against this one user's cold scores, and that is only possible if the trial-level data was kept from the start.
- **Verifier correction:** One sub-clause is misattributed. 'Wider spacing yielded better learning but significantly longer practice RTs' is not this paper's own result — it is their summary of Yan et al., and they describe that result as 'a very wide spacing interval provided comparable (or slightly higher) memorial benefits than a narrower spacing schedule. However, RTs during practice were significantly longer in the wider spacing schedule.' 'Comparable or slightly higher' is not 'better learning'; in fact the point being made is the opposite of a spacing endorsement — wider spacing cost more time for no clear gain, which is precisely why time cost belongs in the objective function.
- **Verifier notes:** Verified in full (PMC7567101). The 40% figure is real and appears both in the abstract ('up to 40% more items recalled in conditions where practice was scheduled optimally') and in the results ('The OET 94 condition led to approximately 40% higher memory retention than conventional scheduling (S15)'). Read it as a RELATIVE improvement over one particular conventional schedule among the set compared, not an absolute 40 percentage points. Model fit was good (predicted vs actual r = 0.775, p < 0.001; AUC = 0.871). Strength MODERATE: single paper, single lab, simulations plus human experiments, declarative concepts and rule application only, no motor tasks — as the claim itself correctly notes. Two cautions on the implication rather than the claim: (1) 'it needs no population-fitted parameters' is not right — Pavlik-family models do use fitted parameters, and the paper's advantage comes from a fitted model of practice plus a gain-per-time selection rule; for a single user the honest framing is that the SELECTION POLICY (highest expected gain per second) is portable and cheap even if the underlying model is crude or hand-tuned. (2) The core storage recommendation — log raw per-trial (item, correct, latency, timestamp) rows in Room so the policy can be swapped later without losing history — is sound, is independent of whether any of the above effect sizes replicate, and is the single most robust action item in this whole set.

## Adaptive/personalized learning algorithms and difficulty control for a single-user piano foundations trainer

### The "85% rule" is a closed-form result for stochastic-gradient-descent learners on *binary classification with Gaussian decision noise*: the learning-rate-maximising training error rate is ER* = ½[1 − erf(1/√2)] ≈ 15.87%. Precision then grows as √t instead of log t (the "exponential improvement" claim). Crucially, the optimum is a *constant error rate*, not a constant difficulty — the authors explicitly recommend clamping the achieved error rate and letting difficulty float, because optimal difficulty rises as precision rises. The 85% figure is distribution-specific: 82% for Laplacian noise, 75% for Cauchy. The paper's own Discussion states results "apply specifically to binary classification tasks for which stochastic gradient-descent learning rules apply", that not all models have a sweet spot (a Bayesian learner with perfect memory is indifferent to ordering), and that batch learning and multi-category tasks "will likely depend" on further factors.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Wilson, R.C., Shenhav, A., Straccia, M. & Cohen, J.D. (2019). The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning. Nature Communications, 10, 4646.
- **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Analytic derivation plus simulations: single-layer perceptron, multi-layer nets with backprop, and the Law & Gold biologically-plausible model. No human participants; no motor-skill task.
- **Effect size:** ER* ≈ 0.1587 (85% accuracy). Precision β(t) = √(β₀² + 2ηK_f(t−t₀)) under fixed-error training, i.e. √t growth vs log t for fixed difficulty.
- **Transfer to this app:** Partially. The control law transfers cleanly and needs zero data. The specific number 85% does not transfer with confidence: piano note-hitting is not binary classification with Gaussian decision noise, and the paper never tested motor sequence learning. Treat 85% as a defensible default for the *reading/perception* half of the app (interval-reading trainer, notation recognition — genuinely a classification task) and as unvalidated for the motor half.
- **Design implication:** Keep SPEC §3's 85% target but implement it the way the paper prescribes: a *closed-loop error-rate clamp*, not a difficulty ladder indexed to an ability estimate. Concretely, run a per-passage integral controller on measured note accuracy: e_t = (target_success − observed_success over last N notes); adjust the single scalar "difficulty knob" d ← d + k_i·e_t, where d maps monotonically onto the decomposition ladder already in the spec (tempo × hand-split × bar-count × scaffold-on/off). Do not try to estimate an ability parameter and then pick difficulty from it — the paper's result says the error rate is the invariant, the difficulty is not.
- **Verifier notes:** Every element checks out, several verbatim. Confirmed from the paper: 'ER*=½[1−erf(1/√2)]≈0.1587'; for the fixed-error-rate schedule 'β(t)=√(β0²+2ηKf(t−t0))', i.e. 'precision grows as the square root of time', versus logarithmic growth at fixed difficulty, which the authors themselves label 'exponentially slower' / 'exponential improvements in the rate of learning'. Confirmed: 'the optimal difficulty changes as a function of precision' (Fig 1c) and the recommendation that 'optimal learning can be achieved by clamping the error rate during training at a fixed value'. Laplacian 82% / Cauchy 75% confirmed. All four Discussion caveats confirmed. Simulations confirmed as perceptron, two-layer net on MNIST with backprop, and the Law & Gold model; no human participants. The reporting agent did not overstate this paper — it even imported the paper's own limitations. Strength is capped at MODERATE, not STRONG, because this is a single analytic result validated only by its own authors' simulations, with zero human data and no motor-skill task. Note the '√t vs log t' framing is a slower-than-exponential improvement in the ordinary sense; the word 'exponential' is the authors' own usage and the claim correctly quarantines it in scare quotes.

### When the same fixed-error-rate logic is applied to an actual motor skill, the optimum lands near 68% success (≈31.7% error), not 85%. Participants trained at clamped success rates of 85%, 70%, 50% and 35% on a difficulty-adaptive Pong task; learning (measured on a common post-test) peaked around 30% error and was *worse* at 85%. The authors derive ~31.73% from a model in which motor learning is driven by continuous error magnitude rather than binary outcome — i.e. an easier schedule starves the learner of the error signal that drives adaptation.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Al-Fawakhiri, N., Kayani, S. & McDougle, S.D. (2023). Evidence of an optimal error rate for motor skill learning. bioRxiv 2023.07.19.549705.
- **URL:** https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705
- **Peer reviewed:** False
- **Sample / design:** n = 184 adults, between-subjects, 4 clamped-success-rate conditions (85/70/50/35%), ~70 practice trials with online paddle-size adaptation, plus a computational model. Adults, single session — no long-term retention test.
- **Effect size:** Optimal error rate 31.73% → target success 68.27%. Learning at 85% success was reliably below the ~70% and ~50% conditions.
- **Transfer to this app:** Directly relevant — adults, motor skill, difficulty-clamped design, closest task class in the literature to piano passage practice. But it is a preprint with no located peer-reviewed version as of July 2026, one session, and a laboratory Pong task; treat as the best available evidence rather than settled. Flag it in the code comment as such so the constant is easy to revisit.
- **Design implication:** Split the difficulty controller's target by skill type instead of using one global 85%. Store the target as a per-skill-class constant: motor/execution skills (tempo, hand independence, leaps, evenness) → target ≈ 0.70 success; perceptual/reading skills (interval reading, notation→key mapping) → target ≈ 0.85. This is a one-line config change in the controller from finding 1 and costs nothing to run. Log which target was active per attempt so a later N-of-1 comparison is possible.
- **Verifier correction:** Learning was NOT measured on a common post-test. It was a 'Learning Index' (LI) = the average baseline-corrected inverse-RMSE (β) across all 70 TRAINING trials, i.e. improvement during practice on each participant's own adaptively-sized paddle, corrected against a 10-trial constant-difficulty baseline. Also, only one of the three group contrasts reached significance: 30% error beat 15% error (t(89)=3.11, p=0.003, d=0.65), but was NOT significantly better than the 50% error group (t(94)=0.98, p=0.32, d=0.20) and only marginally better than 65% (t(87)=1.58, p=0.12, d=0.33). Accurate version: in a single-session online Pong task, training at ~30% error produced more within-practice improvement than training at ~15% error; the data cannot distinguish 30% from 50% error, so a 0.70 success target is one defensible point in a wide 0.50–0.70 band, not an identified optimum.
- **Verifier notes:** The citation is real and most numbers are right: four groups with imposed error rates of 15/30/50/65% (equivalently 85/70/50/35% success), 70 training trials with trial-by-trial paddle-size adaptation, theoretical optimum derived as 'ER* = 1 − erf(1/√2) ≈ 0.3173 ... an optimal training schedule should aim to maintain performance at a 68.27% success rate', and a model explicitly motivated by motor errors being 'signed and continuous (rather than binary and unsigned)'. The n discrepancy resolves in the claim's favour: N=194 collected, 'The final experimental sample was 184 subjects' after exclusions. TWO REAL PROBLEMS. (1) There is no post-test in this study at all — the claim's parenthetical is fabricated. (2) The 68% optimum is not statistically separable from 50% success, and the authors themselves disown the group comparisons. Strength is WEAK: preprint after ~2.5 years, single session, no retention test, outcome measured during training under the manipulated variable, and the load-bearing continuous analysis is only R=0.25 (p=0.001). This does not support a specific 0.70 target; it supports 'somewhere in 50–70% success beats 85%'.

### The only large field experiment that manipulated target error rate directly found the optimum depends on which outcome you optimise, and that short-term engagement points the *opposite* way from learning. Four conditions targeted 5%, 20%, 35% and 50% error. Easier practice won on short-term survival (proportion still answering after k questions: C5 89.2% vs C50 81.2%, CI ±0.77%), but harder practice won on long-term engagement (return rate after >10h: C5 15.2%, C20 16.0%, C35 16.6%, C50 16.8%, CI ±0.75%) and on learning — power-law learning rate k on unbiased periodic "reference questions" was higher for the harder conditions, the biggest gap being C5 vs everything else. By 100 answers survival had converged (26.0–26.5%).

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Papoušek, J., Stanislav, V. & Pelánek, R. (2016). Impact of Question Difficulty on Engagement and Learning. Proc. Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS 2016), 267–272. (Full version: Tech. Report FIMU-RS-2016-02, Masaryk University.)
- **URL:** https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/its-target-difficulty.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Randomised trial in a live adaptive geography-practice system (outlinemaps.org): ~3,300,000 answers from ~37,000 self-selected learners, Nov 2015–Jan 2016, 10 contexts analysed. Declarative knowledge, not motor. Attrition bias acknowledged by the authors. Contradicts earlier Math Garden and Lomas et al. results, which found easier was better in maths/game domains.
- **Effect size:** Short-term survival spread ~8 percentage points (89.2%→81.2%) favouring easy; return rate spread ~1.6 pp favouring hard; learning-rate k favouring hard, largest difference between 5% error and all others.
- **Transfer to this app:** The metric-selection lesson transfers fully and is arguably the single most useful finding here for one user, because with n=1 you cannot afford to optimise the wrong number. The specific direction (harder is better) is from declarative fact learning and conflicts with the maths/game literature, so do not use it to override findings 1–2 on the level of the target itself.
- **Design implication:** Two concrete moves. (a) Ramp the target: make session 1 and the warm-up of every session deliberately easy (~90–95% success) to clear the activation-energy bar SPEC §3.1 cares about, then let the work zone run at the harder per-skill target from findings 1–2. (b) Never tune the difficulty controller against within-session metrics (notes played, session completion, "did he keep going") — this study shows those proxies systematically select for too-easy practice. Tune it against next-session performance on the same passage and against the spaced-review pass rate, which is the app's only unbiased learning probe. That means the between-session batch pass in SPEC §8a should compute and store a per-passage "reference attempt" outcome: first attempt of a passage on a later day, before any adaptation kicks in.
- **Verifier notes:** Every single number is exact, from the report's Table 1: C5 89.2%/15.2%, C20 87.0%/16.0%, C35 84.0%/16.6%, C50 81.2%/16.8%, survival CI ±0.77%, return-rate CI ±0.75%. Two refinements for the reader: 'return rate' is defined as probability of returning after a delay of more than 10 hours, exactly as claimed; and the harder conditions C35/C50 stopped hitting their target error rate after ~30 questions in many contexts, so the top-end contrast degrades over the session. Attrition bias is acknowledged by the authors, as the claim states. MODERATE not STRONG: enormous n but a single study, one domain (declarative geography), self-selected learners, and it contradicts rather than replicates the prior Math Garden / Lomas findings.

### Elo is the recommended lightweight skill tracker for adaptive practice — θ ← θ + K(R − P), P = 1/(1+e^{−(θ−d)}) — with two well-established refinements: replace constant K with an uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1+bn) (fitted values a=1, b=0.05) so early estimates move fast and later ones stabilise, and use asymmetric updates γ for correct / δ for incorrect (the "PFAE" variant), because an attempt is both evidence *and* a learning opportunity. PFAE outperformed both standard PFA and BKT on predictive accuracy. But the item-difficulty half of Elo is population-powered: Pelánek states plainly that "the system needs at least 100 students to get good estimates of item difficulty", and simple proportion-correct — which looks adequate under random item selection — *fails* under adaptive selection. Bolsinova et al. add a sharper pathology: when item difficulties are calibrated on the fly *and* selection is adaptive on the current ratings, rating variance inflates without bound instead of converging, because selection depends on the current estimation error rather than the true parameter; their fix is "parallel Elo", two independent rating chains per person and item where each chain's updates are driven by items selected using the *other* chain (≈2× data cost).

### The widely repeated claim that BKT is "unidentifiable" is wrong as stated — Doroudi & Brunskill show Beck & Chang's (2007) example was not an identifiability failure, and that under mild parameter conditions BKT *is* identifiable as a hidden Markov model. The real and unfixed problem is **semantic model degeneracy**: the maximum-likelihood parameters are frequently inconsistent with BKT's own conceptual meaning (e.g. guess > 0.5, or slip+guess ≥ 1, implying "knowing" the skill lowers success probability). They demonstrate this arises systematically when BKT is fitted to data generated by a *different* true learning process — which is always the case in practice. Separately, Clement et al. state the operational consequence bluntly: BKT-style models "have many parameters, and identifying all such parameters for a single student is a very hard problem due to the lack of data, the intractability of the problem, and the lack of identifiability of many parameters."

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Doroudi, S. & Brunskill, E. (2017). The Misidentified Identifiability Problem of Bayesian Knowledge Tracing. Proc. 10th Int. Conf. on Educational Data Mining (EDM 2017). (Best-paper nominee.)
- **URL:** https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~shayand/papers/EDM2017.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Theoretical analysis of the BKT HMM plus synthetic-data experiments fitting BKT to data generated by alternative learning models. No human sample.
- **Effect size:** Not an effect-size paper. Result is qualitative: identifiability holds under mild conditions; degenerate (semantically invalid) MLE fits are common and predictable.
- **Transfer to this app:** Strongly applicable as a prohibition. The honest read is that BKT's parameter-learning machinery is unusable at n=1, but BKT-as-a-fixed-parameter-Bayesian-filter is a perfectly good cheap mastery estimator. Note also that BKT assumes no forgetting, which is wrong for motor passages — the memory model in finding 9 must own retention, not BKT.
- **Design implication:** Do not fit per-skill 4-parameter BKT (L₀, T, G, S) from one learner's attempt history. If you want a P(mastery) number for the mastery gate in finding 12, use BKT with *fixed, hand-set, semantically constrained* parameters — e.g. G = 0.10, S = 0.15, T estimated from a single pooled learning-rate fit across all skills, L₀ from the passage's feature-derived difficulty — and update only the latent P(L) per skill. That is a 1-degree-of-freedom filter, is stable on 20 observations, and its output stays interpretable. Enforce the constraints G+S < 1 and G < 0.3 as assertions in the `:core` unit tests so a future "let's optimise these" change cannot silently produce a degenerate model.
- **Verifier notes:** Confirmed from the abstract: 'We show that the problem they pointed out was not an identifiability problem, and using an existing result from the identifiability of hidden Markov models, we show that under mild conditions on the parameters, BKT is actually identifiable' and that semantic model degeneracy is 'the model parameters that best fit the data are inconsistent with the conceptual assumptions underlying BKT'. The degeneracy conditions are confirmed and slightly sharper than the claim states: Baker et al.'s theoretical degeneracy is guess OR slip > 0.5, and the paper shows the first form of empirical degeneracy 'is equivalent to P(G)+P(S)>1', which does imply that a student is more likely to answer correctly when they have NOT mastered the skill. The paper also flags that P(G) < 0.5 as a degeneracy criterion 'does seem somewhat arbitrary depending on the domain' while defending P(S) > 0.5 as genuinely degenerate — mildly relevant to the proposed G < 0.3 assertion. The Clement quote is verbatim: 'Typically, these models have many parameters, and identifying all such parameters for a single student is a very hard problem due to the lack of data, the intractability of the problem, and the lack of identifiability of many parameters.' TWO OVERSTATEMENTS worth flagging, neither of which changes the engineering conclusion. First, the paper is weaker than 'demonstrate this arises systematically': its own words are 'We give some intuition for why semantic model degeneracy may arise by showing that BKT models fit to data generated from alternative models of student learning CAN have semantically degenerate parameters.' Second, 'which is always the case in practice' is the reporting agent's editorial addition, not a claim the paper makes. Also note an internal tension the claim does not surface: Clement et al. support their sentence by citing Beck & Chang (2007) for 'lack of identifiability' — i.e. the corroborating quote propagates precisely the error Doroudi & Brunskill correct. Cite Clement for the data-scarcity and intractability half, not for the identifiability half.

### How you summarise practice history matters more than which model family you pick, and *recency-weighted* summaries win. Against AFM (raw count of prior practice) and standard PFA (separate counts of prior successes and failures, Pavlik, Cen & Koedinger 2009), Galyardt & Goldin systematically compared representations of recent practice — counts, box (sliding-window) kernels, and exponential-decay kernels — and found that an **exponentially-decayed proportion of recent successes** gives the highest predictive accuracy. They package this as Recent-Performance Factors Analysis (RPFA).

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Galyardt, A. & Goldin, I. (2015). Move Your Lamp Post: Recent Data Reflects Learner Knowledge Better than Older Data. Journal of Educational Data Mining, 7(2), 83–108. Base model: Pavlik, P.I., Cen, H. & Koedinger, K.R. (2009). Performance Factors Analysis — A New Alternative to Knowledge Tracing. Proc. AIED 2009, 531–538.
- **URL:** https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/JEDM100
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Model comparison on intelligent-tutor response logs, cross-validated predictive accuracy across several representations of practice history. Population-level fitting; the *representation* result is what transfers, not the fitted coefficients.
- **Effect size:** Exponential-decay proportion-of-successes beat count-based (AFM/PFA) and box-kernel representations on held-out predictive accuracy.
- **Transfer to this app:** The recency representation transfers completely and is the cheapest win in this list — it needs no cross-learner data, is O(1) per attempt, and directly serves the forgetting/drift behaviour a motor-skill app must model. The remediation-target *selection* is the part that needs the uncertainty guard, since with tiny counts the argmax over ~8 error channels is mostly noise.
- **Design implication:** Make the exponentially-decayed success proportion the canonical per-skill state in the Room schema, not cumulative counts: s ← λ·s + (1−λ)·outcome, with one float per skill and λ ≈ 0.8–0.9 (tune λ offline once against the reference-attempt outcomes from finding 3). This is a single multiply-add per attempt — zero-allocation, safe for the hot path — and it also gives the difficulty controller its observed-success signal and the drift-detection the app needs after a layoff. Extend the same decayed-proportion state to *error types* rather than only skills: keep one decayed rate per diagnostic channel already recorded in SPEC §8a (wrong-note-above, wrong-note-below, early-onset, late-onset, missing-note, extra-note, per-hand), and drive remediation by ranking channels on decayed rate × a hand-set remediation cost. Be honest about the limit: the confusion-matrix / bug-library / Q-matrix-refinement literature (Learning Factors Analysis and successors) all diagnose *which* misconception a learner holds by pooling across learners, and none of it is available at n=1. For one user, error-pattern diagnostics should be descriptive statistics with an explicit uncertainty guard — a Beta(1,1)-updated rate per channel, reported to the user only once its 95% credible interval excludes the all-channel mean, so the blunt-metrics readout in SPEC §2.8 never asserts a "weakness" that is 6 attempts of noise.
- **Verifier notes:** The paper's abstract states the claim almost word-for-word: 'We investigate several representations of recency, such as the count of prior practice in the AFM model, and the proportion of recent successes with exponential and box kernels. We find that an exponential decay of a proportion of successes provides the summary of recent practice with the highest predictive accuracy over alternative models. As a secondary contribution, we develop a new logistic regression model, Recent-Performance Factors Analysis...' Box kernels are confirmed as sliding windows weighting the most recent K attempts equally. Three precisions on the design, which the claim's SAMPLE/DESIGN describes only loosely. (1) It is ONE real-world dataset, not several: the mastery-learning 'Skill Builder' feature of ASSISTments, supplemented by simulated datasets (the authors' stated reason for the simulations is that real datasets have replicability limitations). (2) The primary model-selection metric is AIC, defended as equivalent to cross-validation via Stone (1977), with student-stratified cross-validation discussed — so 'cross-validated predictive accuracy' is a fair but loose gloss. (3) The ASSISTments data are extremely sparse per student (median 3 skills per student, median 4 attempts per skill), which cuts both ways: it is a poor analogue for one learner with thousands of attempts, but it does mean the recency result was not an artifact of long histories. The paper's own name for the model is 'R-PFA'. Separately, note that the large block in this claim's IMPLICATION about confusion matrices, bug libraries, Q-matrix refinement and Learning Factors Analysis being unavailable at n=1 is unsourced editorial reasoning, not a finding of Galyardt & Goldin — plausible, but do not treat it as cited.

### An IRT-based difficulty controller can be made to work on very small data by (a) parameterising difficulty over *item attributes* instead of per-item, and (b) discounting past interactions so the ability estimate can move during learning — dropping IRT's constant-ability assumption. - **URL:** https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2023/proceedings/2023.EDM-posters.54/index.html
- **Peer reviewed:** True

### Item/activity selection can be driven by *empirical learning progress* with no student model, no cognitive model and no pre-training. ZPDES treats each activity type as a bandit arm whose reward is the recent increase in success rate — the success rate over the last d/2 attempts minus that over the preceding d/2 — so arms that are already mastered and arms that are impossible both score zero reward, and the sampled set is confined to a "ZPD" defined by a coarse expert prerequisite graph, advanced when a bandit's weight plus an absolute success threshold ω are met. In a 400-child school study ZPDES matched or beat an expert-authored sequence (e.g. 95% of ZPDES students succeeded at least one hard "R"-type exercise vs 75% for the expert sequence, χ² p < 0.05, confirmed by ANOVA), and notably the *less* informed algorithm (ZPDES) beat the more informed one (RiARiT) with real students even though RiARiT won in simulation — the authors read this as evidence that population-wide parameters hurt when the goal is personalisation.

- **URL:** https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1115278.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True

### The trainable memory models behind modern spaced repetition are calibrated on enormous corpora, and their published parameter values — not their fitting procedures — are what a small deployment can use. Half-life regression (HLR) fits log₂ half-life as a linear function of practice-history features and cut recall-prediction MAE by >45% against baselines on Duolingo data. The DSR/DHP lineage that FSRS descends from was built from 220 million MaiMemo review logs, modelling memory as (difficulty, half-life/stability, retrievability) with a Markov property, and its stochastic-shortest-path scheduler — which minimises expected total reviews to reach a target memory state rather than greedily hitting a retention threshold — improved on prior state of the art by 12.6%.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · MODERATE
- **Citation:** Settles, B. & Meeder, B. (2016). A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning. Proc. ACL 2016, 1848–1858; and Ye, J., Su, J. & Cao, Y. (2022). A Stochastic Shortest Path Algorithm for Optimizing Spaced Repetition Scheduling. Proc. ACM SIGKDD (KDD '22), 4381–4390.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3534678.3539081
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Settles & Meeder: ~13M Duolingo practice sessions, regression on lexeme-level features. Ye et al.: 220M MaiMemo review logs, Markov memory model + SSP optimisation, deployed online. Both are population-scale; neither reports per-user parameter recovery from small histories.
- **Effect size:** HLR: >45% MAE reduction vs baselines (also reported on AUC and Spearman correlation of estimated half-life). SSP-MMC: 12.6% improvement over prior SOTA in review cost.
- **Transfer to this app:** The models transfer as *pretrained priors*, and this is the honest framing — using defaults is not a compromise, it is the correct use of population-scale calibration at n=1. Two real caveats: both are declarative-memory models (vocabulary), and motor-skill retention has a different decay profile with a large consolidation/sleep component; and both assume item-level independence, whereas piano passages share fingering and harmonic material, so per-passage half-lives will be correlated in ways the model cannot see. Expect the scheduler to be useful but its absolute interval predictions to be poorly calibrated for the first months.
- **Design implication:** Ship FSRS with its published default parameters and treat per-user parameter optimisation as a late, guarded, offline-only step. Concretely: (a) keep the default weights compiled in; (b) in the between-session batch pass, once a passage-review history exceeds a few hundred graded reviews, re-fit only the 2–4 most identifiable parameters (initial stability per grade, and the difficulty-decay term) by minimising log-loss, with strong box constraints and a rollback if held-out log-loss does not improve against the defaults; (c) never re-fit all 17–19+ weights from one learner. Second, adopt the SSP framing rather than a fixed desired-retention knob: the scheduler's objective should be "minimise total reviews to reach and hold a target stability for this passage", which is exactly the finish-state logic SPEC §3 wants. Third, and specific to this app: the "grade" must be the measured accuracy+tempo ratio versus target (as SPEC §3.3 already says) — HLR/FSRS lag features assume a self-report or binary recall signal, so define a deterministic mapping from (accuracy, tempo ratio) to the 4 FSRS grades and freeze it, because a drifting grade definition silently corrupts the whole review history.
- **Verifier notes:** Both verified, largely verbatim. Settles & Meeder's abstract: 'reducing error by 45%+ compared to several baselines at predicting student recall rates', with Table 2 showing HLR MAE 0.128 against Leitner 0.235, Pimsleur 0.445 and logistic-regression 0.211, and the text confirming 'they also reduce MAE compared to Leitner by least 45%'. Ye et al.'s abstract confirms all three of the claim's elements at once: 'We have collected 220 million students' memory behavior logs with time-series features and built a memory model with Markov property. ### The established answer to "how do you personalise when you have almost no data on this learner" is hierarchical Bayes: put a population-level prior over individual parameters and let each learner's estimate shrink toward it in proportion to how little data that learner has. Combining that with an explicit psychological memory model (the DASH family — item difficulty and student ability parameters plus summary features of the study history at multiple timescales) produced a 16.5% boost in end-of-semester cumulative-exam retention over massed study and, critically, a 10.0% improvement over a *one-size-fits-all spaced* schedule — the latter being the clean estimate of what personalisation itself bought.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · STRONG
- **Citation:** Lindsey, R.V., Shroyer, J.D., Pashler, H. & Mozer, M.C. (2014). Improving Students' Long-Term Knowledge Retention Through Personalized Review. Psychological Science, 25(3), 639–647.
- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797613504302
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Semester-long randomised within-subject study in a real middle-school Spanish course, retrieval-practice software, cumulative exam after semester end. Children/adolescents, declarative vocabulary, classroom setting.
- **Effect size:** +16.5% retention vs massed study; +10.0% vs generic spaced study. The 10.0% is the personalisation-specific increment.
- **Transfer to this app:** The mechanism transfers fully and is the right skeleton for a single-user app; the empirical numbers do not (adolescents, vocabulary, classroom). The one genuine limitation is that a true hierarchical model needs a population to *estimate* the prior, which Fingerly lacks — so priors must be hand-set from published values and treated as assumptions, with the shrinkage weight (σ²₀) chosen deliberately to encode how much you trust each literature-derived default. Setting σ²₀ large for the motor-vs-perceptual target-success split (finding 2, a preprint) and small for the FSRS defaults (finding 9, 220M observations) is the correct calibration of confidence.
- **Design implication:** This is the design pattern for every learner-model parameter in the app, and it resolves the cold-start problem stated in SPEC §8a directly. For each parameter (per-skill learning rate, ladder step size, optimal passage length, per-skill target success, FSRS overrides), store a hand-authored prior mean and variance derived from the literature values in this review, and report/act on the posterior mean = shrinkage-weighted blend of prior and this user's data: θ̂ = (n·x̄/σ²_obs + μ₀/σ²₀)/(n/σ²_obs + 1/σ²₀). Two consequences that are easy to get wrong: (i) the blunt-metrics readout in SPEC §2.8 should display the *posterior with its interval*, so "left-hand leaps: −1.2 ± 1.1" reads as honestly uncertain rather than as a measured fact; (ii) the §4a preference model's SGD-on-swipes should be regularised toward a zero prior (L2 / MAP rather than plain SGD), which is the same shrinkage in disguise and is what keeps 20 swipes from producing a confident nonsense weight vector.
- **Verifier correction:** One sub-clause is wrong. Rowland's initial-test-type moderator (Table 1) is: cued recall g = 0.61 [0.52, 0.69], k = 104; FREE recall g = 0.29 [0.07, 0.52], k = 36; recognition g = 0.29 [0.10, 0.47], k = 19. Free recall was identical to recognition, not better. The correct statement is 'CUED recall > free recall = recognition.' Also, 'material complexity' was not a directly coded moderator; the claim is an inference from the stimulus-type moderator (prose 0.58, paired associates 0.59, single words 0.39, other 0.27).
- **Verifier notes:** Article verified via Crossref and full text (PDF). Headline figures are verbatim: g = 0.50 (CI [0.42, 0.58]), Z = 12.38, p < .001, 159 effect sizes. Feedback moderator verbatim: no feedback g = 0.39 (k = 107) vs feedback g = 0.73 (k = 52), QB = 18.72, p < .01. Retention interval: <1 day 0.41, >=1 day 0.69. Abstract confirms 'Limited support was found for existing theoretical accounts attributing the testing effect to enhanced semantic elaboration.' Unreported caveats: high heterogeneity (tau^2 = 0.21, Q = 1009.42); published g = 0.58 vs unpublished g = 0.25 (a publication-bias signal); and the 'retrievability' moderator shows no-feedback studies with <=50% initial recall success had g = 0.03 [-0.21, 0.27] — i.e. the effect vanishes when retrieval mostly fails without feedback. That last point strengthens the app implication about always giving feedback. The design implication (produce, don't recognise; don't narrow the answer space) survives the correction, since cued recall 0.61 > recognition 0.29 is the relevant contrast for a cue->key drill.

### Logistic-regression learner models with well-chosen features beat deep knowledge tracing on datasets of moderate size or with many interactions per student; DKT only leads on very large datasets or where fine-grained temporal ordering carries most of the signal. This held across an extensive comparison on nine real-world datasets.

- **Status:** CONFIRMED · STRONG
- **Citation:** Gervet, T., Koedinger, K., Schneider, J. & Mitchell, T. (2020). When is Deep Learning the Best Approach to Knowledge Tracing? Journal of Educational Data Mining, 12(3), 31–54.
- **URL:** https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Empirical comparison of logistic-regression feature sets (IRT/AFM/PFA/DAS3H-style) against DKT and variants on nine public ITS datasets of varying size and interactions-per-student. Population-level; no single-user regime tested.
- **Effect size:** Qualitative crossover result: logistic regression leads on moderate-size datasets and on datasets with very high interactions-per-student; DKT leads on large-size datasets and where precise temporal information dominates.
- **Transfer to this app:** Applies as a clear negative recommendation (no DKT/SAKT/transformer knowledge tracing), which is the useful kind. The caveat is that the study's "moderate size" is still thousands of students, so it does not directly certify logistic regression at n=1 — it certifies that the deep alternative has no case here, and findings 4–7 supply the positive argument.
- **Design implication:** Settles the model-family question for `:core` without needing an experiment: implement the learner model as a small logistic/Elo-family predictor (findings 4, 6, 7) and do not put a sequence model on the device. Fingerly's data profile is the extreme end of the regime where logistic wins — one student, very high interactions per student, tiny total size. Practical form: P(success on next attempt) = σ(θ_skill − d_features + β₁·s_decayed + β₂·log(1+Δt) + β₃·tempo_ratio), with β's hand-initialised and refit offline in the between-session pass by plain IRLS on a few hundred rows. That is exact, deterministic, runs in milliseconds on CPU, and matches SPEC §8a's "classical statistics on CPU" note — the NPU stays reserved as the spec says.
- **Verifier notes:** All six figures verified verbatim from the abstract: cognitive g=0.49, 95% CI [0.30, 0.69], k=19, N=1686; motivational g=0.36 [0.18, 0.54], k=16, N=2246; behavioural g=0.25 [0.04, 0.46], k=9, N=951. The stability claim is supported and slightly stronger than the paper's own hedge: the abstract says the cognitive effect 'was stable in a subsplit analysis of studies employing high methodological rigor' while motivational and behavioural effects 'were less stable' — but the paper's motivational subsplit is unambiguous, g=0.22, SE=0.17, p=0.20, 95% CI [-0.11, 0.56], k=7, N=1063, i.e. non-significant. So 'did NOT remain stable' is fair for motivational; for behavioural the paper's own word is 'less stable' and the claim's stronger 'did NOT' is an extrapolation from k=9 down to a smaller rigorous subset. Caveats: mixed ages, general education populations, no ADHD sample, and behavioural rests on only k=9 with a CI nearly touching zero. The IMPLICATION is well-supported and converges with claim 7: invest in mechanics that STRUCTURE learning, be sceptical of decorative motivational layers. The alignment with the project's SPEC ban on praise/cheerleading copy is genuine — the motivational layer is demonstrably the least replicable part of gamification. This is the only claim in the set whose evidence is both strong and directly about a design decision.

### The conventional mastery-learning cutoff in intelligent tutors — stop drilling a skill when P(mastery) > 0.95 — is not optimal, and the error is in the direction of stopping too early. Categorising knowledge estimates into eight mastery bands and relating them to performance on the *subsequent* lesson, higher thresholds such as 0.98 yielded additional benefits in both performance and learning downstream. This cuts against the opposite well-known finding that Cognitive Tutor curricula contained substantial over-practice detectable by Learning Factors Analysis — the two together imply the threshold is a real tunable with costs on both sides, not a safe constant.

- **Status:** MISCHARACTERIZED · WEAK
- **Citation:** Zhang, J., Vanacore, K., Baker, R.S., Ch, N., Mills, C. & Henkel, O. (2025). How Much Mastery is Enough Mastery? The Relationship between Mastery in a Lesson and the Performance on the Subsequent Lesson. Proc. 18th Int. Conf. on Educational Data Mining (EDM 2025), 427–433. Contrast: Cen, H., Koedinger, K. & Junker, B. (2007). Is Over Practice Necessary? — Improving Learning Efficiency with the Cognitive Tutor through Educational Data Mining. Proc. AIED 2007.
- **URL:** https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED675652.pdf
- **Peer reviewed:** True
- **Sample / design:** Zhang et al.: observational analysis of log data from Rori, an online foundational-mathematics tutor; mastery estimates binned into 8 levels and regressed against next-lesson performance. Observational (not randomised), maths domain, school-age learners. Cen et al.: LFA-driven curriculum redesign in Cognitive Tutor geometry, with a controlled comparison.
- **Effect size:** Zhang et al. report directional benefit of 0.98 over 0.95 on subsequent-lesson performance and learning; the paper is a 7-page short paper and does not establish an optimum. Cen et al. showed measurable over-practice removable without loss of learning.
- **Transfer to this app:** Applies as a caution against inheriting 0.95 uncritically, but the specific numbers are from school maths and an observational design, and neither paper concerns motor skill. For piano the behavioural gate is on much firmer ground than any threshold on a modelled P(mastery), especially given finding 5's conclusion that per-user BKT parameters are not estimable. The genuine cost of a stricter gate — over-practice, which Cen et al. show is real, and which collides with SPEC §2.7's 80%-cliff abandonment risk — is why (c), the no-further-progress condition, must be in the conjunction: it is what prevents a stricter mastery bar from turning into grinding.
- **Design implication:** Make the mastery gate a two-sided, explicit, logged criterion rather than a single probability threshold — and prefer *behavioural* criteria over a modelled probability, since at n=1 the probability is the least trustworthy quantity in the system. Recommended composite gate for a passage: (a) ≥3 consecutive attempts at or above target accuracy *at target tempo*, (b) decayed success rate ≥ 0.9, (c) the ZPDES-style learning-progress reward has decayed to ~0 (no further gain available — finding 8), and (d) one successful *spaced* re-attempt ≥1 day later (the reference attempt from finding 3). Requiring (d) is the cheap way to buy the 0.98-style stringency the Zhang result argues for, because it tests retention rather than within-session performance, and it costs nothing extra since the SRS pass already schedules it. Store the gate's four booleans per passage so the criterion can be retuned retrospectively against history — with one user, retrospective re-scoring of a logged criterion is the only form of A/B test available.
- **Verifier correction:** Two overstatements. (1) The paper does not conclude 0.95 'is not optimal'; its abstract says 'while the widely adopted threshold of 0.95 remains relevant, higher thresholds, such as 0.98, yield additional benefits'. (2) 'The error is in the direction of stopping too early' is a causal claim the authors explicitly refuse: 'As is the nature of any correlational analysis, the relationship identified in the current study does not prove causation. Although it is possible that attaining a higher mastery level caused students to perform better on subsequent lessons, it is also plausible that the students who attain high mastery have characteristics that make them more likely to perform better on both lessons.' They call for causal methods and for replication on other platforms and domains. Accurate version: in observational data from one maths tutor, students whose end-of-lesson BKT estimate reached ≥0.98 performed and learned better in the next lesson than those in the 0.95–0.98 band; whether raising the threshold would cause that gain is untested, and selection on student ability is an acknowledged rival explanation. This is a reason to prefer the claim's own behavioural composite gate — especially the spaced next-day re-attempt, which tests retention rather than a modelled probability — over any specific numeric P(mastery) cutoff.
- **Verifier notes:** Citations are exact, including the page range and the six-author list, and the mechanics are right: BKT estimates at the end of each micro-lesson N binned into eight mastery buckets (<0.3, 0.3–0.5, 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.8, 0.8–0.9, 0.9–0.95, 0.95–0.98, ≥0.98), related via multilevel models with random intercepts for students and problems to performance and learning in lesson N+1, on log data from Rori. The claimed tension with Cen et al. is real and is the paper's own framing: 'much of the over-practice identified by [8] may not be over-practice at all', where over-practice 'can account for up to 58% of practice if the system is not optimized'. So the bottom-line design advice — treat the threshold as a genuine two-sided tunable rather than a safe constant, and log the criterion so it can be re-scored retrospectively — survives intact. What does not survive is the directional certainty. Strength WEAK: observational log analysis, no randomisation, one platform, one domain (foundational maths), school-age learners, short paper, and no test of whether forcing students to 0.98 would produce the association.

## Changes this evidence produced

- The drill measures the wrong construct, so no scheduling change matters until that is fixed. Notes fall at the target key's x-position with the note name printed on them, the key lights up, and every white key is labelled — `find-C` can be mastered by tracking a rectangle. Rowland's testing-effect meta-analysis (g = 0.50, 159 comparisons, adults, declarative cue→response material) is both the largest and the most directly applicable effect in the whole digest, and its moderator (recall > recognition) says this is currently a restudy condition, not a test.
- Mastery should be COUNTED, not estimated. `emaAcc` seeds on its first observation, so one correct rep reads as 100% certainty; an all-correct EMA equals exactly 1.0, so the 0.92 threshold is never actually estimated and the criterion collapses to '6 correct prompts' — satisfiable in one massed 45-second block. Rawson & Dunlosky measured the optimum directly (n = 533 adults): 3 correct recalls in the initial session, then 1 in each of 3 spaced later sessions. Two integers replace a fragile estimator.
- Do NOT port the existing FSRS engine to foundations, even though `Fsrs.kt` is sitting right there and note-name recall looks like a flashcard. Karpicke & Bauernschmidt showed schedule SHAPE is nearly irrelevant once retrieval success is equated — total elapsed spacing and cross-session repetition carry the benefit — and FSRS's 17 weights come from 220M reviews with no demonstrated per-user recovery from small histories. A distinct-days counter captures the available benefit at a fraction of the complexity.
- Overlearning past criterion within a session is empirically inert for this material (Rohrer & Taylor: 9 problems vs 3 changed nothing at 1 or 4 weeks; redistributing the same reps across two sessions nearly doubled 4-week retention). The app should actively REFUSE extra reps of a criterion item — which is the opposite of what an engagement-optimising design would do, and is also the cheapest possible session finish state.
- The codebase's own docs criticise foundations for being blocked rather than interleaved, but the evidence points the other way for this material class. Brunmair & Richter found interleaving NEGATIVE for word-like cue→response pairs (g = −0.39, blocked better), and every music-domain contextual-interference study either failed or reversed. Light spacing to separate repetitions of an item, not aggressive shuffling.
- Feedback must be split by latency, and the current design has it exactly backwards — it shows the answer BEFORE the response. Sensory/corrective feedback belongs immediately after the attempt (Rowland's feedback moderator); evaluative aggregates belong after the repetition, a few seconds later (Gabay: adults with ADHD learned WORSE with 0s evaluative feedback than with 3-6s delay). Both agree: never a per-note verdict during play.
- What bites is perceived cost at the decision to start, not reward size (Chong's within-subject drug crossover), and delay is discounted steeply and age-invariantly (Jackson & MacKillop, N = 3,913, d = 0.43). So: state the effort quantum on the button, put the whole payoff inside the sitting, and never build a streak.
- At n = 1 with dozens of observations per atom, every model-fitting approach in the literature is out of reach — Elo's item side needs ≥100 students, adaptive selection plus on-the-fly calibration provably diverges, and BKT's ML fits are semantically degenerate. The only durable investment is the raw per-trial log, which foundations currently does not have at all: no timestamps, no rows in Room, just one opaque aggregate string.
- Three live bugs silently corrupt every number the trainer produces, so they are not cleanup — they are prerequisites: wrong presses are attributed to the NEXT prompt (marking it not-first-try before it is shown); unmeasured latencies return 0ms and `mastered()` accepts `0f..2500f`, so unmeasured counts as maximally fast; and the checkpoint bar `firstTry * 100 / 14 >= 93` is integer division, so the real pass mark is 14/14.

The 3-spaced-days criterion IS a stronger retention test than one 14-prompt run, so the ritual is redundant.
- **Citation:** Rawson, K. A. & Dunlosky, J. (2011). Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough? ## Ideas rejected on evidence

Kept deliberately: re-proposing these without new evidence is a regression, not an improvement.

### Add a second, lower target success rate (~70%) for motor/execution skills alongside 85% for perceptual ones

Rests on Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle (2023), which is a bioRxiv PREPRINT — single session, no retention test, adaptive Pong rather than a musical or recall task. Independently, foundations atoms are declarative cue→response items, not motor sequences, so even if the preprint holds it addresses a different item class. Keeping one target (the 85% already in SPEC §3) is simpler and better supported.

### Aggressively interleave the letter atoms to create contextual interference

Brunmair & Richter's multilevel meta-analysis (59 studies, 238 effect sizes, adults) found interleaving significantly NEGATIVE for word-like cue→response material (g = −0.39, favouring blocked), which is exactly what letter→key items are. The music-domain evidence is worse: blocked practice retained BETTER at 24h in advanced violinists (n = 19), blocked vs random showed no difference in beginning clarinettists (n = 41), and the one pro-interleaving music study had n = 10 with inter-rater reliability r = 0.02-0.59. The current near-blocked schedule is closer to correct than docs/LEARNING.md claims; only light spacing is warranted.

### Port FSRS (or SM-2) to foundations atoms, reusing the existing Fsrs.kt with per-atom stability and due dates

Superficially the obvious move, but Karpicke & Bauernschmidt showed interval SHAPE is nearly irrelevant once retrieval success is equated, and Karpicke & Roediger found expanding schedules actively LOSE at 2-day delay. The published FSRS weights come from 220M MaiMemo reviews with no reported per-user parameter recovery from small histories, so they cannot be personalised here. A 'K successes on distinct days' rule captures the available benefit for two integers instead of a 17-weight model plus card table plus grade mapping.

### Ship a first-launch rhythm calibration (paced tapping at 450/600/750ms plus a beat-alignment probe) to derive a per-user timing window

Puyjarinet et al. is genuine adult evidence (d = 1.20 / 1.39), but n = 21 adults, and 31-38% of ADHD participants fell within the control range. More decisively, foundations is untimed wait-mode key-finding by design — there is no timing dimension for the calibration to parameterise — and a calibration screen is exactly the decision-menu-and-ceremony-before-playing that SPEC §2 bans. If it belongs anywhere it is the song side's `HitJudge` window, not here.

### Fit a proper learner model for the atoms — Elo per skill with U(n) uncertainty decay, or a constrained BKT P(mastery), or attribute-based IRT

Pelánek states plainly that the item-difficulty half of Elo needs at least 100 students; Bolsinova et al. showed that adaptive selection combined with on-the-fly calibration makes rating variance inflate without bound rather than converge (their fix costs 2x the data); and Doroudi & Brunskill showed ML-fitted BKT parameters are routinely semantically degenerate when the true process differs from BKT, which it always does. With 10 atoms and dozens of observations, a criterion counter dominates all of these and is auditable by hand.

### Track the coefficient of variation of response latency (SD/mean) per atom as an automaticity signal

Segalowitz & Segalowitz's speed-up-vs-automatization distinction is real but the evidence is MODERATE and non-motor, and a stable SD needs far more samples per atom than this learner will generate in a month — a CV computed from 5-8 observations is noise wearing a statistic's clothes. A median latency over the last 5 unaided correct trials gets the robustness benefit for a fraction of the state. Revisit once the change-6 trial log has real volume.

### Variable-ratio / intermittent celebration schedules or a streak counter to build persistence

The loot-box argument is popular-press, not established here: Hulsbosch et al. (93 ADHD vs 73 TD children) found NO slower extinction after partial reinforcement — ADHD participants actually made FEWER responses during extinction, so the partial-reinforcement extinction effect did not protect them. Streaks are separately ruled out by steep age-invariant delay discounting (Jackson & MacKillop, N = 3,913) plus the mental-health-app retention collapse (median 30-day retention 3.3%): a streak is a delayed reward whose loss is felt immediately, i.e. all downside. The app has none today; keep it that way.

### Add an opt-in pink-noise or steady-tone background stimulation track to the practice mode

The meta-analytic effect is small, actively NEGATIVE for non-ADHD listeners (g = −0.212), and the stochastic-resonance mechanism has been directly challenged (a pure 100Hz tone worked as well). Decisively, SPEC forbids audio synthesis for live play and this would introduce a competing audio path near the hot loop for a small, contested effect on a task that is untimed and silent by design.

### Surface cumulative on-instrument minutes as the long-horizon progress metric, with a ~500-1000 minute first milestone

The dose evidence (Jamey 2024) is children-only, with the strongest RCTs in 4-8 year olds and no adult samples; the parallel game-based meta-analysis found total dose (15-2835 min) was a NON-significant moderator; and Duke, Simmons & Cash found retention rankings had no significant relationship with practice time, trials, or complete trials. Surface segments-brought-to-criterion instead — which change 7 already does.

### Give each error class its own particle colour/shape (differential-outcomes reward signalling)

De Meyer et al. is children only (ages 8-12) and the transfer to adults is an inference the authors do not make. The render layer already flashes the wrong key red for WRONG_FLASH_NANOS, which is the bulk of the identity-bearing signal, so the marginal gain over existing behaviour is small relative to touching paints in the hot draw path. Change 2's landmark reveal delivers the same 'which thing went wrong' information through a mechanism that is also pedagogically load-bearing.

### Render a redundant staff-notation glyph alongside the 'Press C4' prompt as multimodal presentation

The only accommodation with specific empirical support in Lovett & Nelson's review was oral/read-aloud delivery, in younger students; 'redundant multimodal presentation' via a staff glyph is an analogy to that finding, not the finding itself. It also adds a second notation system to learn during the one drill whose entire purpose is letter→key retrieval, which raises load exactly where the load-dependence review says music/enrichment hurts. Notation belongs in SPEC §4's acquisition module on already-mastered material.

### Bucket sessions into 5-minute blocks and trigger an activity switch when accuracy, timing SD or miss rate degrades past a personal noise band

The two best adult studies flatly disagree — Tucha found significantly steeper deterioration over 20 minutes on three of four measures, Fuermaier tested eight instruments from 6.5 to 33 minutes and found no differential time-on-task effect at all, concluding deficits 'may not depend on task duration'. The digest's own instruction is not to hardcode a cadence from an assumed decrement. Change 7's criterion-based stop ends the sitting on a measured goal instead, with no fatigue detector to build, tune, or debug.

### Immediately re-prompt a missed item within the same drill (Duke-style 'address the error at the point of failure')

Duke, Simmons & Cash is n = 17 elite piano majors, observational, on a difficult three-measure MOTOR passage — the digest's own reading is that motor passages get massed correction loops while declarative items get spacing. Massing an immediate re-attempt of a keyboard-geography item is precisely the drop-after-correct / zero-lag condition that Karpicke & Roediger showed loses at delay. Change 2 delivers the corrective information (reveal the landmark) and the item is requeued later in the drill, not immediately.

## Adversarial critique of the change plan

An independent critic reviewing the plan for gaps, over-engineering, backfires and internal contradictions. Retained because several points are still open work.

## 1. The mechanism the plan missed entirely

**It never touches the HUD, which is a live per-note evaluative verdict plus a streak counter, drawn every frame during every foundations drill.**

`/home/user/fingerly/app/src/main/java/com/fingerly/app/highway/NoteHighwayView.kt:465-466` unconditionally draws `hudLine` and `perfLine`. `rebuildHud()` (621-629) builds `acc <pct>  hit N  miss N  extra N  combo N`, and `HitJudge.accuracyPercent()` counts extras, so the percent visibly drops on every wrong key, and `combo` resets to 0 on every extra. `FoundationsScreen` sets `waitMode`, `leadInMs`, `tapToRestart` and nothing else — there is no HUD flag, so the drill screen shows a falling accuracy percent, a miss/extra count, a live streak, and an fps/dropped-frames line.

That is precisely the immediate-evaluative condition Gabay et al. (2018) found *impaired* adult ADHD probabilistic learning (r = −0.38 to −0.39 with symptom severity), it is loss-framed against Aster et al. (2024), and it is a streak — which the plan's own rejected-ideas list asserts "The app has none today; keep it that way." That claim is factually wrong about the codebase. keyInsight #6 states the rule ("never a per-note verdict during play") and no change in the plan implements it.

The positive half of that mechanism is also missing: nothing renders progress toward the finish state *during* the drill. Plichta & Scheres' anticipation-hyporesponsiveness result (d = 0.48–0.58) says the finish state must be a continuously updating on-screen quantity, and Marx/Mette say elapsed-remaining must be externalised non-numerically. Right now a drill gives no indication of how many prompts remain. Change 7 puts the count on the *home* screen, i.e. after the reward is already collected.

Concrete fix, cheaper than any change in the plan: add `var hudMode = HUD_FULL/HUD_NONE`; in foundations draw a depleting 8-segment prompt bar plus a "N to criterion" fill at the top, and move accuracy/miss/extra/combo to the post-drill summary only.

Two runners-up. (a) **No lag constraint on prompt order.** `prompts.shuffle(rng)` (`FoundationsTrainer.kt:169`) can place two same-atom prompts adjacent, and the focus atom is 5 of 8 prompts, so massing is the norm. Karpicke & Roediger's active ingredient was *delaying the first retrieval*; Donovan & Radosevich's largest effect (d = 1.22) was sub-minute ITI on simple psychomotor items. The plan cites both in its rejections and implements neither. A spacing-aware shuffle (no atom within k=2 positions; intro-tip atom's first prompt placed after ≥2 intervening items) is one pure function in `:core`, unit-testable. (b) **`spacedDays` credit is not a cold probe.** Change 5 increments on any unaided hit on a new day, which can be the 6th prompt after three reveals. Walker/Kuriyama and the Papoušek reference-attempt implication both require the day's *first* attempt. One-line change; large construct-validity gain.

## 2. Over-engineered or ceremony he will resent

- **Change 9 (5-note run + median inter-note gap as a fingering proxy).** MIDI cannot see fingers. The gap proxy is defeated by rolling two fingers fast, and it will also flag a correctly-placed hand that pauses to think. Don't build it. Delete `RH_POSITION`/`LH_POSITION` and their two atoms (`FoundationsTrainer.kt:272-273, 303-314`), which also drops the mastery gate from 10 atoms to 8. If hand placement needs teaching, it is a one-time animation with no ✓.
- **Change 10's 5-slot latency ring + median, while simultaneously loosening the threshold to "fail open."** You are adding serialized state and a migration for a criterion deliberately set to gate nothing. Change 6 already logs `latencyMs` per prompt to Room. Compute the median offline; keep zero in-memory latency state beyond the raw log.
- **Change 8 (placement test).** 14 unaided prompts, no reveal, as the first thing a learner who *already failed this course* sees. Kromann's result is about testing *inside* a course, not a cold gate. It is also a decision menu before playing.
- **Change 11's `tipFor` branching.** Keep the decayed error rates (they feed the log); drop the tip-selection engine. Show each atom's `introTip` exactly once, ever. A two-sentence prose block chosen by a diagnostic rule is the thing he stops reading in week one, and change 2's landmark reveal already carries the same information visually.

## 3. Plausible backfires

- **The combination of 1 + 3 + 5 + 8 is the plan's most dangerous property.** It makes the drill much harder, makes per-atom mastery take ≥4 calendar days, keeps `allMastered()` as the gate to songs, and deletes the "Skip basics" escape (`FoundationsScreen.kt:183`). For 10 atoms that is weeks before he touches a song, for a learner who abandons at 80%. Ungate the song side from foundations outright, or gate it on 3 atoms (find-C, find-F, find-G) and let foundations run as a warmup alongside.
- **The 4-second reveal becomes the dominant strategy.** A revealed prompt costs nothing: no criterion hit, but no penalty either, and the drill still advances. With patience he completes every drill at zero recall. Requeue revealed prompts unaided at the end of the drill and make the drill's own finish count unaided-only.
- **Change 1 turns the lead-in and every inter-prompt gap into a blank screen.** `leadInMs = 1500` plus `toScore`'s 2.0 s spacing (`FoundationsTrainer.kt:323`), and `waitPrompt` is drawn only while `waitingNow` (NoteHighwayView 467-472). Suppress the falling rectangle and you get 1.5 s + 7 × 2 s ≈ 15 s of black screen with no cue per 8-prompt drill. That is Low et al.'s delay-aversion budget spent on nothing, before he has played a note. Cut spacing to ~0.3 s and show the prompt text at prompt onset, not at clamp.
- **Change 7's null return locks him out.** Rohrer's result is about repetitions of the *same item*, not about ending the sitting. Tucha-vs-Fuermaier and Hupfeld both point to *switching activity*, not terminating. When every atom is at session criterion, hand off to the song side or free play; do not close the door on a rising bout.
- **Change 5 deletes the only named terminal event the course has.** "Checkpoint passed" is bad as a *gate* (the integer-division bug makes it 14/14) but it is the finish state. Replace it, don't just remove it.
- **rung 3 = C2** is where his hands never go, and change 1 removes the white-key letters, leaving the middle-C dot as the sole landmark 21 white keys away. Cap the ladder at rung 2.

## 4. Internal contradictions

1. **Change 2 censors the very measurement change 4 exists to fix.** Revealed prompts contribute no latency sample, so the median-of-5 in change 10 is a median over survivors: the worse he gets, the more slow trials are discarded, and the *faster* his measured latency looks. Change 4 removes fabricated 0 ms and change 2 immediately installs survivorship bias in its place. Also, any latency threshold ≥ `revealAfterMs` (4000) is unreachable by construction, while change 10 wants to *raise* `masteryLatMs` above 2500.
